\section{Background}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Existing Selectivity Metrics and Their Comparison}
\label{sec:related:metrics}

Several metrics have been proposed to quantify kinase inhibitor selectivity
from profiling panel data.
Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} introduced the selectivity score (S-score),
defined as the fraction of tested kinases bound with a dissociation constant
below a fixed cutoff.
Uitdehaag and Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} proposed the selectivity entropy,
which treats the normalized binding profile as a probability distribution and
computes its Shannon entropy.
Graczyk\cite{Graczyk2010} adapted the Gini coefficient from economics, treating
the distribution of a compound's activity across the panel as an inequality
distribution.
Klaeger et al.\cite{Klaeger2017} introduced the concentration- and
target-dependent selectivity (CATDS) score, which quantifies the reduction in
binding to a given target relative to the summed reduction across all detected
targets at a specified inhibitor concentration.

Bosc et al.\cite{Bosc2017} subsequently proposed two further metrics, the
window score and ranking score, and compared them against four previously
published metrics (the S-score, Gini coefficient, selectivity entropy, and a
partition index) on the Davis and Anastassiadis datasets. They showed that
compound rankings differ substantially depending on the metric applied, computed
several metrics at more than one parameter value (S-score thresholds, window
widths, ranking positions), and noted that certain compounds cannot be scored
under some definitions when threshold conditions are not satisfied. For example, ratio-based scores are undefined
for a compound with no kinase above the activity threshold, since there is then
no primary target or off-target to compare.

To our knowledge, attempts to reconcile the definitions have been limited.
Miljkovic and Bajorath\cite{Bajorath2018} compared CATDS-based selectivity
assessments from Klaeger et al.\ with activity data mined from ChEMBL for the
same compounds, finding broad consistency between the two sources.
The KInhibition platform\cite{KInhibition2018} addressed conflicting results across metrics by introducing its own composite score (combining on-target
inhibition with an off-target inhibition penalty).
More recently, the same group built a KIRHub resource\cite{Gujral2026}
on a
new profiling campaign of 86 approved kinase inhibitors against 758 kinases
and disease-associated mutant variants. This resource offers users a choice
between a further
named metric (the KISS score, an on-target/off-target balance) and a separate
Gini-based score.

To our knowledge, how much each parameter and implementation choice moves the
rankings across datasets and assay technologies, and which profile properties
determine when the definitions agree, have not been characterized. The present
work addresses both.

\subsection{Axiomatic Approaches to Ranking and Measurement}
\label{sec:related:axiomatic}

Precedent for an
axiomatic characterization of measurement functions is established.
Shannon\cite{Shannon1948} demonstrated that the entropy of a probability
distribution is uniquely determined, up to a multiplicative constant, by three
requirements: continuity in the probabilities, monotonic growth with the
number of equally likely outcomes, and consistency under grouping of outcomes.
Khinchin\cite{Khinchin1957} subsequently gave an alternative axiomatization
(continuity, maximality on the uniform distribution, expansibility, and
additivity over independent subsystems).
The selectivity entropy of Uitdehaag and Zaman applies the Shannon formula to
normalized binding profiles but provides no analogous axiomatic justification.
The axioms that single out Shannon entropy, such as maximality on the uniform
distribution and additivity over independent subsystems, characterize a measure
of uncertainty, and none of them was chosen with selectivity in mind.

Axiomatic analysis can also show that no function meets a set of
requirements. Arrow\cite{Arrow1951} proved this for social welfare functions,
and Singleton and Booth\cite{Singleton2022} for a subset of candidate axioms
for truth discovery, the problem of identifying true facts from conflicting
reports. Of the three properties we state for selectivity, no measure we
examine meets the second, and several simple constructions meet the other two
(Results).

To our knowledge, this axiomatic style of analysis has not previously been
applied to any selectivity or promiscuity measure in drug discovery.
The present work proposes empirically motivated properties as a
foundation for such an analysis.
