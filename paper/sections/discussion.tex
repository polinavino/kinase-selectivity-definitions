\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central finding of this work is that existing kinase inhibitor selectivity
definitions, placed on a common log-affinity scale, do not measure the same
quantity.
The ratio definition and the distribution-based family (S-score, entropy, Gini)
produce substantially different compound rankings, and
the largest disagreements are concentrated in identifiable kinds of profiles.
This has practical consequences: a compound that ratio-based analysis designates
as selective may be designated as moderately promiscuous by entropy-based
analysis, and vice versa, depending on the shape of its binding profile. Among compounds that clear the
activity threshold, the two families disagree most on broad profiles (Type~3,
Results).

This coexistence may have a historical explanation.
In classical pharmacology and analytical chemistry, selectivity is inherently
pairwise. IUPAC formalizes it as a selectivity coefficient, the ratio of
binding constants for an analyte and an interferent,\cite{IUPAC2001} and
ratio measures follow that tradition.
Kinome-wide profiling, however, poses a distributional question: how
concentrated is a compound's binding across hundreds of kinases?
Entropy and Gini address this question directly.


\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

Pending a formal resolution, three practical recommendations follow from our
findings.
First, D1 (reliability threshold) should be adopted as a reporting
standard.
Compounds with no binding above the $\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$ activity threshold
should not receive selectivity scores, or should have their scores explicitly
flagged as unreliable.
Of the 222 Klaeger compounds, 16 (7.2\%) fall into this category (Type~1). All 16 bind above the assay detection floor of $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$
(maximum affinities 5.16--6.00), so the gate sits at the activity threshold.

Second, selectivity assessments should report how rankings change with
parameter choice, alongside the score at the chosen parameter value. Rank
standard deviation across the parameter sweep is a compound-specific,
interpretable quantity that identifies which compounds' assessments are robust
to parameter choice, analogous to a confidence interval in statistical
estimation. This rank standard deviation is not a guide to robustness across
platforms: on the 46 compounds profiled in both Davis and Klaeger, it does not
predict how far a compound's rank moves between the two ($|r| \leq 0.25$).

Third, when comparing selectivity across studies that use different definitions,
the comparison should be restricted to the subset of compounds and parameter
values for which the definitions agree.
Within the distribution-based family, the S-score, entropy, and Gini correlate
at $r \geq 0.72$ on the full panel (Table~\ref{tab:correlations}). They give similar rankings
when parameters are chosen consistently and panels have similar, near-complete
coverage. On smaller panels the Gini diverges on Davis, Klaeger, and
Anastassiadis because of its convention for undefined scores (Results).
Ratio-based measures, in turn, should not be compared directly with
distribution-based measures at any panel size without acknowledging the
conceptual distinction.

\subsection{Limitations}

The analysis covers four profiling datasets spanning three assay technologies
and two readout types, but these still represent a fraction of the chemical and
kinase space relevant to drug discovery.
The assays report on different scales and detect different shares of active
interactions (Methods).
The two-family structure (in our common log-affinity operationalization)
replicates across all four datasets, and so, with the qualifications given in
Results, do the two instability relations tested on all four (ratio--gap and
entropy--$n_\mathrm{active}$). This indicates that the findings are not
artifacts of a single assay platform. On Anastassiadis, however, the
two-family separation comes mainly from compounds with no active kinase, and
on Metz it partly reflects coverage (Results).
Findings should still be interpreted in light of
these technological differences.

Our comparison is also restricted to four widely used, historically
established definitions. Bosc et al.'s window score and ranking
score\cite{Bosc2017} were not included. On the same four datasets both
correlate strongly with the ratio (Spearman $r = 0.80$--$0.96$), so they do not
form a third cluster.
The CATDS score\cite{Klaeger2017} was also not implemented directly, since it requires raw
concentration-response data. A proxy computed from our baseline-subtracted
affinity profiles correlates far more strongly with Gini and entropy than with
$R(k)$ on all four datasets (Spearman $0.91$--$0.99$ against Gini and
$0.82$--$0.96$ against entropy, against $0.45$--$0.70$ for $R(k)$, Supporting
Information Table~S2).
We did not add it as a fifth measure, because the distribution-based family is
already represented by three members.

A further axis of platform dependence that this analysis does not examine is
cell-based versus cell-free profiling. Recent work shows that selectivity
classifications for the same compounds can differ substantially between
biochemical binding panels and cellular target-engagement assays. For example, several
type II (DFG-out-binding) inhibitors show cellular interactions that
cell-free assays miss~\cite{Binder2026,Binder2026correction}.
A plausible contributor, not established by that work, is that the cellular
assays report occupancy of full-length kinase in intact cells, with regulatory
domains, binding partners, and intracellular ATP present. Many
cell-free binding panels, instead, score isolated, recombinantly expressed
kinase-domain constructs. This specific contrast does not map cleanly onto the
two cell-free datasets used here. In particular, Davis profiles recombinant constructs (on
the same competition-binding platform, Karaman et al.\ used catalytic domains for
large multidomain kinases and full-length constructs for small single-domain
ones\cite{Karaman2008}), while Klaeger's Kinobeads approach captures endogenous,
full-length kinases directly from cell lysate.

We compared how much the choice of assay technology and the choice of
selectivity definition each move a compound's selectivity ranking. We
resolved 46 compounds profiled in both Davis (competition binding) and
Klaeger (chemoproteomics) by matching PubChem CIDs against Klaeger's drug
names, and computed each definition's
median rank on this fixed set of 46 compounds (separately on each platform).
Rankings agree less across platforms than across definitions, so the
\emph{platform effect} (same definition, Davis rank vs.\ Klaeger rank) is
on average the larger of the two: Spearman $r = -0.06$ to $0.69$ across the four
definitions for the platform comparison (mean $0.44$), against $r = 0.14$ to
$1.00$ for the within-platform, cross-definition comparison (mean $0.60$).
The ratio definition is the extreme case: its Davis ranking and its Klaeger
ranking for the same 46 compounds are statistically indistinguishable from
uncorrelated ($r = -0.06$, $p = 0.67$). This should temper any
cross-study comparison of selectivity rankings regardless of which definition
is used.

All four existing definitions and the candidate measure introduced above
treat a compound's affinity
vector as an unordered multiset. Thus, two compounds with identical affinity
distributions receive identical scores regardless of which specific kinases
carry the off-target activity. A compound whose off-targets are
pharmacologically inert and one whose off-targets are known safety-relevant
kinases (e.g.\ a kinase implicated in a class toxicity) are therefore
scored identically if their affinity distributions match. None of the measures
discussed here incorporates target identity, and none accounts for exposure.
A measured affinity alone does not establish whether a target is occupied in
a patient, which also depends on the drug concentration achievable at a
tolerated dose. A selectivity score is consequently, at best, a summary of
\emph{binding breadth}, and go/no-go decisions should weigh it alongside
target-specific liability knowledge and exposure modeling.

We also examined whether in vitro selectivity predicts clinical toxicity,
correlating the selectivity rankings of the FDA-approved Klaeger compounds with
FAERS adverse-event rates ($n = 46$) and FDA-label discontinuation rates
($n = 33$).
None of the rate-based outcomes were significantly associated with selectivity
under any definition (all $|r| < 0.17$, all $p > 0.27$).
The only nominal signal is a weak correlation between raw FAERS report counts and
the ratio ranking ($r = 0.31$, $p = 0.035$), which may reflect prescription
volume rather than toxicity.
We do not read the absence of a rate-based association as evidence that
selectivity and toxicity are unrelated. FAERS report counts likely track how
often a drug is prescribed, and label discontinuation depends on the patient
population treated for each indication, so neither can isolate the effect of
selectivity.
A proper test would require indication-stratified, treatment-related
adverse-event rates from controlled trials with matched comparators, which are
not available in standardized form across these drugs.

The candidate's distributional consistency rests on fixing its activity
boundary at the assay floor $x_0$, and in none of the four datasets is $x_0$ a
measured detection limit. On Davis and Klaeger it is the value assigned to pairs
with no detected binding, on Metz the lowest reported value (Methods), and on
Anastassiadis a $20\%$ inhibition
noise level that we set. Fixing $x_0$ therefore removes the analyst's choice
only to the extent that each dataset's encoding of non-detection is accepted as
given. Where that encoding is itself uncertain, the candidate's standing on D3
is no better than the entropy's.

Finally, D3 is assessed empirically, on four datasets. Whether the
properties are jointly satisfiable only by measures within the candidate's
family (Supporting Information, Section~S5), and whether a uniqueness result
analogous to Shannon's holds, are left for future work. A complementary direction is a consensus ranking
that does not commit to any single measure, that is, a partial order on which
all existing measures agree.

The four properties are also unlikely to be the complete set, and this work
suggests further candidates that we do not assess here. A measure should be
correctly oriented against a crude external proxy for the concept, such as the
number of active kinases, since a measure can satisfy D2 to D4 and still rank
promiscuous compounds as selective. A measure should declare its scale type,
because the four definitions report on incomparable scales and
Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity} shows that leaving the operationalization undeclared
can change a ranking substantially. The panel-size behaviour reported above
could itself be stated as a property. Stability under measurement error in the
affinities is another candidate, which the present data cannot test. Agreement
across datasets profiling the same compounds can be tested, as the platform comparison above does.
Klaeger and Anastassiadis share 19 compounds by name, but a comparison between an affinity assay and a
single-concentration inhibition assay is harder to interpret. Metz identifies
compounds only by internal ID and PubChem SID, and we did not resolve those
against the other datasets.
