\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our characterization shows that existing kinase inhibitor selectivity
definitions, placed on a common log-affinity scale, do not measure the same
quantity.
The ratio definition and the distribution-based family (S-score, entropy, Gini)
produce substantially different compound rankings, and
the largest disagreements are concentrated in identifiable kinds of profiles.
This has practical consequences: a compound that ratio-based analysis designates
as selective may be designated as moderately promiscuous by entropy-based
analysis, and vice versa, depending on the shape of its binding profile. Among
compounds that clear the activity threshold, the two families disagree most on
broad profiles (Type~3, Results).

This coexistence may have a historical explanation.
In classical pharmacology and analytical chemistry, selectivity is inherently
pairwise. IUPAC formalizes it as a selectivity coefficient, the ratio of
binding constants for an analyte and an interferent,\cite{IUPAC2001} and
ratio measures follow that tradition.
Kinome-wide profiling, however, poses a distributional question: how
concentrated is a compound's binding across hundreds of kinases?
Entropy and Gini address this question directly.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

Pending a formal resolution, four practical recommendations follow from our
findings.
First, D1 (reliability threshold) should be adopted as a reporting
standard.
Compounds with no binding above the $\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$ activity threshold
should not receive selectivity scores, or should have their scores explicitly
flagged as unreliable.
Of the 22 such Klaeger compounds (Type~1), 16 bind some kinase above the floor
of $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$, so only a gate at the activity threshold excludes
them.

Second, the activity boundary should be fixed in the analysis protocol, before
the data are examined, and selectivity assessments should report how rankings
change when it is moved. No measure we examined satisfies D2. Rank standard
deviation across the parameter sweep is a compound-specific summary of this
sensitivity, and its value depends on the range and spacing of the sweep. It
is not a guide to robustness across datasets: on the 46 compounds profiled in
both Davis and Klaeger, it is not associated with how far a compound's rank
moves between the two ($|r| \leq 0.23$).

Third, when comparing selectivity across studies that use different definitions,
the comparison should be restricted to the subset of compounds and parameter
values for which the definitions agree.
Within the distribution-based family, the S-score and our log-affinity entropy
and Gini correlate at $0.90$--$0.999$ among compounds with an active kinase on
Davis and Klaeger (Results); their published forms do not (Methods). They give
similar rankings when parameters are chosen consistently and panels have
similar, near-complete coverage. On smaller panels the Gini diverges on Davis,
Klaeger, and Anastassiadis because of its convention for undefined scores
(Results). Ratio-based measures, in turn, should not be compared directly with
distribution-based measures at any panel size without acknowledging the
conceptual distinction.

Fourth, a selectivity analysis should state its unit of analysis and, for small
panels, how many compounds remain scorable. Counting mutant constructs or
complex subunits as separate targets changes the rankings (Supporting
Information, Section~S6), and on random 50-target panels $12$--$29\%$ of the
compounds on Davis, Klaeger and Anastassiadis have no active target.

\subsection{Limitations}

The analysis covers four profiling datasets spanning three assay technologies
and two readout types, but these still represent a fraction of the chemical and
kinase space relevant to drug discovery.
The assays report on different scales and detect different shares of active
interactions (Methods).
The two-family structure (in our common log-affinity operationalization) is
clear only on the two $\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets, and on Klaeger only among
compounds with an active kinase. On Anastassiadis the separation comes mainly
from compounds with no active kinase, and on Metz it largely reflects coverage
(Results). The two instability relations tested on all four datasets
(ratio--gap and entropy--$n_\mathrm{active}$) replicate with the
qualifications given in Results, and on Metz the entropy relation may also
reflect coverage (Methods). Findings should be interpreted in light of these
technological differences.

Our comparison is also restricted to four widely used, historically
established definitions. Bosc et al.'s window score and ranking
score\cite{Bosc2017} were not included. The ranking score is $R(k)$ at a
shifted rank (Methods), and the window score correlates strongly with the ratio
on the same four datasets (Spearman $r = 0.91$--$0.96$), so neither forms a
third cluster.
The CATDS score\cite{Klaeger2017} was also not implemented directly, since it
requires raw concentration-response data. A proxy computed from our
baseline-subtracted affinity profiles correlates more strongly with the Gini
than with $R(k)$ on all four datasets (Spearman $0.91$--$0.98$, against $0.73$ or
less, Supporting Information Table~S2). The proxy lacks the concentration
dependence of the published score, so this does not show how the published
score behaves.

A further axis of platform dependence that this analysis does not examine is
cell-based versus cell-free profiling. Selectivity classifications for the same
compounds can differ substantially between biochemical binding panels and
cellular target-engagement assays. For example, several type II
(DFG-out-binding) inhibitors show cellular interactions that cell-free assays
miss.\cite{Binder2026,Binder2026correction} Davis profiles recombinant
constructs, and Klaeger's Kinobeads approach captures endogenous, full-length
kinases from cell lysate, so neither dataset represents the intact cell.

We compared how much the choice of dataset and the choice of selectivity
definition each move a compound's selectivity ranking. We resolved 46 compounds
profiled in both Davis (competition binding) and Klaeger (chemoproteomics) by
matching PubChem CIDs against Klaeger's drug names, restricted both panels to
the 200 kinases they share, and computed each definition's median rank on this
fixed set of 46 compounds (separately on each dataset). For the same
definition, the Davis and Klaeger rankings correlate at Spearman $r = 0.33$ to
$0.84$ (mean $0.60$), against $r = -0.09$ to $1.00$ between definitions on the
same dataset (mean $0.56$). The choice of definition therefore moves rankings
about as much as the change of dataset. Without restricting the panels to their
shared kinases, the cross-dataset correlations are lower (mean $0.52$), so
part of the difference between datasets comes from which kinases each profiles.
The ratio is the extreme case: on the shared panel its Davis and Klaeger
rankings for the same 46 compounds correlate at only $r = 0.33$ ($p = 0.03$).
This should temper any cross-study comparison of selectivity rankings
regardless of which definition is used.

All four existing definitions and the candidate measure introduced above
treat a compound's affinity
vector as an unordered multiset. Thus, two compounds with identical affinity
distributions receive identical scores regardless of which specific kinases
carry the off-target activity. A compound whose off-targets are
pharmacologically inert and one whose off-targets are known safety-relevant
kinases (e.g.\ a kinase implicated in a class toxicity) are therefore
scored identically if their affinity distributions match. None of the measures
discussed here incorporates target identity, and none accounts for exposure.
A measured affinity alone does not establish whether a target is occupied in
a patient, which also depends on the drug concentration achievable at a
tolerated dose. A selectivity score is consequently, at best, a summary of
\emph{binding breadth}, and go/no-go decisions should weigh it alongside
target-specific liability knowledge and exposure modeling.

We also examined whether in vitro selectivity is associated with clinical
safety, correlating the selectivity rankings of the FDA-approved Klaeger
compounds with the proportions of FAERS reports that were serious, fatal, or
involved hospitalization ($n = 46$) and with FDA-label discontinuation rates
($n = 33$). None was significantly associated with selectivity under any
definition (all $|r| \leq 0.20$, all $p > 0.17$). The only nominal signal is a
weak correlation between raw FAERS report counts and the ratio ranking
($r = 0.32$, $p = 0.03$), which may reflect prescription volume. FAERS
proportions describe reports, not incidence among treated patients, so this
analysis cannot isolate the effect of selectivity on toxicity.

No measure satisfies D2, so each must fix its boundary parameter in the
protocol, at the floor for all but the S-score and the active count. In none of
the four datasets is the floor $x_0$ a measured detection limit. On Davis and
Klaeger it is the value assigned to pairs with no detected binding, on Metz the
lowest reported value (Methods), and on Anastassiadis a $20\%$ inhibition noise
level that we set. Fixing $x_0$ therefore removes the analyst's choice only to
the extent that each dataset's encoding of non-detection is accepted as given.

The D2 sweeps cover only four datasets. Whether D1, D3 and a stated
recoverability target together single out a measure, and whether a uniqueness
result analogous to Shannon's holds, are left for future work. A complementary
direction is a consensus ranking that does not commit to any single measure,
that is, a partial order on which all existing measures agree.

The three properties are also unlikely to be the complete set. A measure should
be correctly oriented against a crude external proxy for the concept, such as
the number of active kinases, since a measure can satisfy D1 and D3 and still
rank promiscuous compounds as selective. A measure should declare its scale type
and its unit of analysis, because both change rankings
(Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}; Supporting Information, Section~S6). Agreement
across datasets profiling the same compounds can be tested, as the comparison
above does. Klaeger and Anastassiadis share 19 compounds by name, but a
comparison between an affinity assay and a single-concentration inhibition assay
is harder to interpret. Metz identifies compounds only by internal ID and
PubChem SID, and we did not resolve those against the other datasets.
