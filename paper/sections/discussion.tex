\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}


Discrepancies between the rankings of the scores compared here 
depend on protocol choices made before any score is computed. Moreover, the 
different ranking computations summarize different aspects of a binding profile. 
The S-score, entropy and Gini agree more with one another than with
the ratio when the entropy and Gini are computed from readouts above a common
baseline and compounds without an active kinase are left out. With
weights inversely proportional to the binding constants, 
or with compounds that have no active kinase
included, this grouping weakens or disappears. When most entries are
coded at the floor, as on Metz, the S-score, entropy and Gini agree for a
different reason: each ranks compounds largely by how many kinases the
compound was tested against. Bosc et al.'s window score correlates
strongly with the ratio ($0.91$--$0.96$), and their ranking score is a ratio
by definition. A proxy for the CATDS score of Klaeger et al., the top target's
share of the profile, instead correlates more with the Gini than with the
ratio on every dataset (Supporting Information, Section~S6). 
The ratio and the S-score, entropy and
Gini disagree most on broad profiles with one dominant target. 
On compounds and kinases shared by two datasets, changing the
definition moved rankings about as much as changing the dataset.

\subsection{What the Design Criteria Guarantee}

D1 is a reporting rule that any score can adopt. D2 and D3 test how a score
changes when the boundary moves and when a weakly bound target is added.
The baseline-subtracted entropy
and Gini fail D2, as does the ratio used here. The published entropy has no
boundary parameter, so D2 does not apply to it. The S-score fails D3 when its
threshold is above the floor. The entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini, and
the published entropy also fail D3. D4 fixes the direction of a score, which D1
to D3 leave open. 

Meeting D2 does not make rankings independent of the boundary, so the
boundary must still be fixed by protocol. D3 concerns adding a weakly bound
kinase, above the floor and at or below the activity threshold. A kinase
added at the floor has zero weight in the gated entropy and leaves its score
unchanged. Adding a kinase at or below the activity threshold cannot raise
the score; most entries fall in this range. Strengthening binding to an
existing target can raise it.

D4 applies only to profiles with equally strong targets. With unequal binding
strengths, a compound can bind more targets yet have most of its binding
concentrated on a few. A score that measures concentration can then rank it as more
selective without violating D4. Correlation with the number of active kinases
on real data therefore does not establish whether a score satisfies D4.

Among the scores studied, only the gated
entropy meets all four and weights targets by affinity. Within the class
defined in Proposition 2, the criteria allow exponential weights and their
equal-weight limit. For finite scales, D3 requires the scale to be at least
the distance between the floor and the activity threshold. On the affinity
datasets, the published entropy with floor-coded pairs given zero weight has
a smaller scale and violates D3.

Two simpler scores give almost the same rankings as the gated entropy: the
entropy with $\beta = x_0$ and the S-score with $\tau = x_0$. The entropy
weights targets by affinity but fails D2 and D3. The S-score meets all four
criteria but weights every target above the operational floor equally. Increasing the gated
entropy's scale reduces weight differences and brings its rankings closer
to those of this S-score. At twice the smallest allowed scale, the gated
entropy retains its affinity weights and recovers rankings from small panels
as fast as the S-score, but does not surpass it.

\subsection{Reporting Recommendations}

Each recommendation follows from a result above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State which targets the score is computed over, including how the
        panel was curated, and the score implementation, including the
        weighting and its parameters, such as a baseline or the gated
        entropy's scale.
  \item Distinguish measured affinities, pairs with no detected binding,
        rejected fits and untested pairs, and state the value and weight each
        receives.
  \item Apply D1: state the activity threshold, withhold or flag the scores of
        compounds with no target above it, and rank the eligible compounds
        among themselves. 
  \item Fix the activity boundary in the analysis protocol and report how
        rankings change when it is moved, for example by each compound's rank
        instability across the sweep. Assess agreement across datasets
        individually.
  \item For small panels, report how many compounds remain scorable as well as
        how well the ranking of those compounds is recovered.
  \item When comparing studies that use different scores, fix the comparison
        protocol in advance and report the compounds on which the scores
        disagree.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

The four datasets, from three assay technologies, cover 68 to 704 compounds and
172 to 363 kinases, and the results may not hold for other compounds or panels.
The datasets also differ in how many interactions they find: the 46 compounds
profiled on both Davis and Klaeger are active against $15.0\%$ of the Davis
panel and $3.9\%$ of the Klaeger panel on average.
On Davis, Klaeger and Anastassiadis every compound was tested against the whole
panel, and only $1.1\%$ of the Anastassiadis entries are missing (set to $0\%$
inhibition). On Metz, each compound was tested against some particular subset of the 172
kinases. 

On no dataset is the floor a measured detection limit. It codes
non-detection (on Davis), non-detection, low-confidence fits and weak measured
affinities (on Klaeger), and untested pairs (on Metz). On Anastassiadis, it is an
operational cutoff for weak inhibition. Fixing the boundary at the floor therefore
depends on how each dataset codes these pairs. Random subpanels are one sampling design,
so panels chosen by kinase family, as many screening panels are, may recover
rankings differently. Cell-free panels can also miss interactions that
cellular target-engagement assays detect\cite{Binder2026,Binder2026correction}.

No score considered here uses target identity or exposure. Two compounds with
the same affinity distribution receive the same score whether their
off-targets are inert or linked to a class toxicity, and an affinity alone does
not establish whether a target is occupied at a tolerated dose. A selectivity
score is at best a summary of the binding profile, to be weighed with
target-specific liability knowledge and exposure modeling. In the exploratory
analysis of 46 approved Klaeger compounds, we detected no statistically
significant correlations between selectivity rankings and the proportions of
their adverse-event reports that were serious, fatal or led to hospitalization
(Supporting Information, Section~S7). This result does not establish an absence
of clinically relevant associations.
