\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The scores compared here summarize different aspects of a binding profile, and
the discrepancies between their rankings depend on choices made before any score is
computed. The S-score, entropy and Gini agree more with one another than with
the ratio when the entropy and Gini are computed from readouts above a common
baseline and compounds without an active kinase are left out. With
association-constant weights, or with compounds that have no active kinase
included, this grouping weakens or disappears. When most entries are
coded at the floor, as on Metz, the S-score, entropy and Gini agree for a
different reason: each ranks compounds largely by how many kinases the
compound was tested against. Ratio measures match the pairwise definition of selectivity in
analytical chemistry\cite{IUPAC2001}, a ratio of binding constants for an
analyte and an interferent. Bosc et al.'s window score also correlates
strongly with the ratio ($0.91$--$0.96$), and their ranking score is a ratio
by definition. A proxy for the CATDS score of Klaeger et al., the top target's
share of the profile, instead correlates more with the Gini than with the
ratio on every dataset (Supporting Information, Section~S6). The ratio and the S-score, entropy and
Gini disagree most on broad profiles with one dominant target. A selectivity ranking is therefore a statement about a
score, its implementation, and a set of compounds and targets, not about the
compounds alone. On compounds and kinases shared by two datasets, changing the
definition moved rankings about as much as changing the dataset.

\subsection{Reporting Recommendations}

Each recommendation follows from a result above.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State which targets the score is computed over, including how the
        panel was curated, and the score implementation, including the
        weighting and any baseline.
  \item Distinguish measured affinities, pairs with no detected binding,
        rejected fits and untested pairs, and state the value and weight each
        receives.
  \item Apply D1: state the activity threshold, withhold or flag the scores of
        compounds with no target above it, and rank the eligible compounds
        among themselves. Otherwise, an active compound's instability depends on
        how the ineligible compounds are scored, as the Gini--skewness
        association showed.
  \item Fix the activity boundary in the analysis protocol and report how
        rankings change when it is moved, for example by each compound's rank
        instability across the sweep. Rank instability is not a guide to
        robustness across datasets: on the 46 shared compounds, a compound's
        rank instability is not associated with how far its rank moves between
        Davis and Klaeger ($|r| \leq 0.18$; Supporting Information,
        Section~S6).
  \item For small panels, report how many compounds remain scorable as well as
        how well the ranking of those compounds is recovered.
  \item When comparing studies that use different scores, fix the comparison
        protocol in advance and report the compounds on which the scores
        disagree.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

The four datasets, from three assay technologies, cover 68 to 704 compounds and
172 to 363 kinases, and the results may not hold for other compounds or panels.
The datasets also differ in how many interactions they find: the 46 compounds
profiled on both Davis and Klaeger are active against $15.0\%$ of the Davis
panel and $3.9\%$ of the Klaeger panel on average.
On Davis, Klaeger and Anastassiadis every compound was tested against the whole
panel, and only $1.1\%$ of the Anastassiadis entries are missing (set to $0\%$
inhibition). On Metz, each compound was tested against some particular subset of the 172
kinases. On no dataset is the floor a measured detection limit. It codes
non-detection (on Davis), non-detection, low-confidence fits and weak measured
affinities (on Klaeger), and untested pairs (on Metz). On Anastassiadis, it is an
operational cutoff for weak inhibition. Fixing the boundary at the floor therefore
depends on how each dataset codes these pairs. Random subpanels are one sampling design,
so panels chosen by kinase family, as many screening panels are, may recover
rankings differently. Cell-free panels can also miss interactions that
cellular target-engagement assays detect\cite{Binder2026,Binder2026correction}.

No score considered here uses target identity or exposure. Two compounds with
the same affinity distribution receive the same score whether their
off-targets are inert or linked to a class toxicity, and an affinity alone does
not establish whether a target is occupied at a tolerated dose. A selectivity
score is at best a summary of the binding profile, to be weighed with
target-specific liability knowledge and exposure modeling. Selectivity rankings
of the approved Klaeger compounds were not associated with adverse-event
report proportions or label discontinuation rates (Supporting Information,
Section~S7).

The four criteria play different roles. D1 is a reporting rule that any score
can adopt. D2 is a diagnostic: no boundary parameter meets it, so the boundary
must be fixed by protocol. D3 is the criterion on which the scores differ, and
the gated entropy's guarantee covers the usual way a panel grows: most kinases
added to a panel bind the compound weakly or not at all, as $83.3\%$ of the
Davis entries and $95.5\%$ of the Klaeger entries lie below the activity
threshold. The guarantee does not extend to strengthening a target already in
the profile. D4 calibrates the direction of a score on profiles with equally
strong targets. Without it, D1 to D3 would not rule out the active count with
its sign reversed. The criteria are necessary conditions, and they do not single 
out one score. The gated entropy is
unique once its weights are fixed, Shannon's
conditions single it out (Proposition 2), but the criteria do not fix the
weights (Results, ``Uniqueness of the Gated Entropy''). On these datasets, the S-score with
$\tau = x_0$ matches the gated entropy on every criterion and in recovery, and
the entropy with $\beta = x_0$ ranks compounds almost identically, although it
violates D3.

How closely a score follows the number of active kinases on real data, such as
the gated entropy's Spearman $-0.78$ to $-0.97$, measures its agreement with
breadth on those data, not whether it is correctly oriented. A score that
measures concentration can correctly rank a compound with more active targets
as more selective when its binding is more concentrated.
