\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:methods:data}

The Davis dataset\cite{Davis2011} comprises binding affinities ($K_d$) for 68
small-molecule kinase inhibitors measured against 433 recombinant kinase
constructs in a competition-binding assay.
Affinity values are reported as dissociation constants and were converted to
$\mathrm{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d / 10^9)$ prior to analysis, following the
convention of the DeepDTA benchmark.\cite{DeepDTA}
The released affinity matrix is complete ($68 \times 433$, 29{,}444 entries),
with all non-binding pairs assigned the floor value
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ (corresponding to a $K_d$ of 10~$\mu$M).
This follows DeepDTA,\cite{DeepDTA} and Bosc et al.\ used nearly the same
value ($10.1~\mu$M).\cite{Bosc2017} It
places non-binding pairs at the floor, above their true, unmeasured affinity.

The released constructs are not one per kinase. They include mutant,
phosphorylation-state and single-domain constructs of the same gene (for
example, 12 EGFR constructs, and BRAF and BRAF(V600E)), 20 lipid-kinase
constructs and three non-human kinases. For 26 of the 68 compounds, the two
best-bound constructs are forms of the same gene. Selectivity across kinases
needs one entry per kinase, chosen without reference to any compound's
affinities. We therefore use one reference construct per human protein kinase
gene: the wild-type construct, or its catalytic or N-terminal kinase domain
where several domains are profiled. Genes profiled only as mutants are
removed. This leaves 363 targets. Results on the released constructs, and with
the best affinity per gene in place of the reference construct, are in
Supporting Information (Section~S6).

Active entries ($\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$, i.e.\ $K_d < 1~\mu$M) make up 16.7\% of the
Davis matrix. This $1~\mu$M cutoff is a common activity threshold in kinase
profiling. With the S-score's threshold sweep shifted up by one log unit (from
$[5.5, 8.0]$ to $[6.5, 9.0]$), the S-score still correlates more with the
entropy and Gini ($0.88$) than with the ratio ($0.54$).

The Klaeger study\cite{Klaeger2017} profiled 243 clinical kinase inhibitors
against the human kinome using Kinobeads chemical proteomics. Its Kinobeads
table lists fitted targets for 229 of them.
Apparent dissociation constants ($K_d^\mathrm{app}$) were derived from
dose--response curves across eight inhibitor concentrations in cancer cell
lysates.
Only high-confidence measurements (as annotated by the original authors) were
retained, yielding 5{,}123 drug--target pairs for 222 drugs and 343
targets.
Affinity values were converted to $\mathrm{p}K_d$ using the same formula as
above.
Missing entries, combinations for which no binding was detected, were assigned
the background value $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$.
Measured values below the floor (194 entries, $K_d^\mathrm{app}$ above
10~$\mu$M) were set to the floor.
The 343 targets are the proteins the Kinobeads pulldown quantified, including
kinase-complex subunits, such as cyclins, captured with their kinases. We use
the 217 targets that Klaeger et al.\ annotate as protein kinases and that are
single genes. This leaves out lipid kinases, four protein groups (such as
PRKCE;PRKCH), and Q6ZSR9, an uncharacterized protein without a kinase domain.
It also leaves out BCR and TRRAP, which the source does not annotate as kinases
and flags as possibly co-enriched with ABL1 and CDK8. A table in the code
repository lists every target of each dataset with the reason it was kept or
removed. Results on the released panel are in Supporting Information
(Section~S6).
Active entries ($\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$) make up 4.5\% of the Klaeger matrix.
The 46 compounds profiled in both datasets (Discussion) are active against
$15.0\%$ of the Davis panel on average and $3.9\%$ of the Klaeger panel, so the
difference does not come from the compounds. These data cannot tell whether
it comes from the chemoproteomic assay's detection threshold or from the two
panels profiling different kinases.

The high-confidence filter removes 793 of the 5{,}916 drug--target rows
(13.4\%), and a removed pair is treated as one with no detected binding.
Retaining the removed pairs shifts the Klaeger cells of
Table~\ref{tab:correlations} by at most $0.08$ and leaves the four
definitions' Klaeger panel-size $p^*$ values unchanged (Supporting
Information, Section~S6).

To test whether our findings depend on the assay technology, we added two
further datasets that measure kinase inhibition by different means.
The Anastassiadis dataset\cite{Anastassiadis2011} profiles 178 inhibitors
against 300 kinases in a single-concentration ($0.5~\mu$M) radiometric activity
assay, reported as percent residual kinase activity relative to a solvent
control.
We converted these to percent inhibition ($100$ minus residual activity,
clipped to $[0,100]$, where higher values denote stronger inhibition), with the
$1.1\%$ missing entries set to zero inhibition. Nine kinases are profiled in
two complexes or isoforms (for example, CDK1 with cyclin A and with cyclin B).
We keep the first-listed of each, leaving 291 targets.
The Metz dataset\cite{Metz2011} reports $\mathrm{p}K_i$ affinities for a large
medicinal-chemistry
campaign using the same negative-log scale as $\mathrm{p}K_d$. The value
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$ is the lowest value reported anywhere in the released
data (only $0.17\%$ of measurements), so we assign it to unmeasured
combinations as the floor. Coverage per compound is highly
skewed (a median compound is measured against only 21 of the 172 kinases). Because of this,
we restricted the dataset to the 704 compounds profiled against at least 50 of
the 172 kinases ($\geq 29\%$ coverage). The threshold excludes the most
sparsely profiled compounds while retaining a large enough compound set for
the panel-size and instability analyses. Across
the 704 retained compounds, coverage ranges from $29.1\%$ to $83.1\%$ of the
172 kinases (mean $40.9\%$, median $37.2\%$, i.e.\ 64 of 172 kinases). Thus, for
the median retained compound, most entries are floor-imputed, and fewer than a
quarter of compounds have more than half of their entries measured
(75th percentile $47.1\%$). A sizable minority sit close to the threshold: 23
of 704 ($3.3\%$) have exactly 50 measured kinases, and 170 ($24.1\%$) have 55
or fewer.

Floor imputation has a large effect on Metz. Nearly every measured value
($99.9\%$) lies above the floor, so a compound measured against more kinases
has more kinases with positive weight and looks less selective. Across the 704
compounds, the Spearman correlation between a compound's coverage and its median
rank (rank 1 = most selective) is $0.98$ for the entropy, $0.95$ for the Gini,
$0.76$ for the S-score, and $0.11$ for the ratio.
Part of this is real, since compounds measured against more kinases are also
active against a larger share of the kinases actually measured, but that
association is much weaker ($r = 0.33$). No fully measured subset of useful size
exists (only 7 kinases are measured for at least $80\%$ of the compounds), so we
keep Metz as a fourth dataset but read its distribution-based results, and their
low agreement with the ratio, as largely reflecting coverage.

The four datasets span three assay technologies (competition binding,
chemoproteomics, and functional activity) and two readout types (affinity and
single-concentration inhibition).
Each selectivity definition is computed on the native readout of its
dataset, and every comparison between datasets uses rank correlations computed
within each dataset.
For the percent-inhibition and $\mathrm{p}K_i$ datasets, the S-score threshold and
entropy/Gini baseline grids were shifted to span each dataset's active range
(thresholds over $[50, 90]\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis and
$\mathrm{p}K_i \in [5.0, 7.5]$ on Metz, and baselines over $[0, 30]\%$ and
$[4.0, 5.5]$). The ratio's off-target index $k \in \{1,\ldots,5\}$ needs no
rescaling.

We excluded two further datasets. The Gao screen\cite{Gao2013} has the same
readout type as Anastassiadis, so it would add no new assay technology, and its
raw data are not openly redistributable. The Patricelli
study\cite{Patricelli2011} profiles too few inhibitors for a ranking analysis.

\subsection{Selectivity Definitions and Parameterization}
\label{sec:methods:defs}

We implemented four selectivity definitions, each with parameters whose values
are not standardized in the literature.
Throughout, a compound's binding profile is the vector of its affinities
$\mathrm{p}K_{d,i}$ across the $N$ panel kinases, with higher
$\mathrm{p}K_{d}$ denoting tighter binding.

\paragraph{S-score.}
Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} defined the selectivity score as the fraction
of the panel that a compound binds: the number of kinases bound with a
dissociation constant below a cutoff, divided by the number of distinct kinases
tested. They reported $S(3~\mu\mathrm{M})$ and $S(100~\mathrm{nM})$, for $K_d$
cutoffs of $3~\mu$M and $100$~nM.
We implemented the same fraction, with the cutoff expressed as a threshold
$\tau$ on $\mathrm{p}K_d$:
\begin{equation}
  S(\tau) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{p}K_{d,i} > \tau].
\end{equation}
Here $N$ is the number of kinases in the panel. On Metz this includes kinases
a compound was not tested against, which are imputed at the floor and so count
only in the denominator.
We used $\tau \in \{5.5, 5.75, \ldots, 8.0\}$ (11 values), spanning $K_d$
cutoffs from approximately 3~$\mu$M to 10~nM, which includes both of Karaman
et al.'s cutoffs ($\tau \approx 5.5$ and $\tau = 7$).

\paragraph{Selectivity entropy.}
Uitdehaag and Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} treat the compound's binding across the
panel as a probability distribution and report its Shannon entropy. A profile
concentrated on few kinases has low entropy and is selective.
In the original definition, the weight on each kinase is its association constant
$K_{a,i} = 1/K_{d,i}$ (the share of compound that would be bound to kinase $i$
in an equimolar mixture), so $p_i = K_{a,i}/\sum_j K_{a,j}$.
We implemented the entropy on the same log-affinity scale used by the other
metrics, weighting each kinase by its affinity in excess of a baseline $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
  H(\beta) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i \log_2 p_i, \quad
  p_i = \frac{\max(\mathrm{p}K_{d,i} - \beta,\, 0)}
             {\sum_j \max(\mathrm{p}K_{d,j} - \beta,\, 0)},
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the affinity below which binding is treated as negligible.
This makes $\beta$ an explicit parameter. We swept $\beta \in \{5.0, 5.25, \ldots, 6.5\}$ (7 values),
a narrower range than the S-score's $\tau$ sweep. Raising either parameter
leaves a growing share of compounds with no kinase above it (on Klaeger, 9.9\%
of compounds at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6.0$ and 58\% at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 8.0$), where the S-score becomes 0 and the
entropy and Gini become undefined. The $\tau$ range is wider because it was
chosen to include Karaman et al.'s cutoffs. The baseline $\beta$ has no published
reference points and was kept near the $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6$ activity threshold.
We compare this weighting with the original association-constant weighting
below. % (``Faithfulness to the original definitions'').

\paragraph{Gini coefficient.}
Graczyk\cite{Graczyk2010} adapted the Gini coefficient from economics: kinases
are sorted by activity and the cumulative fraction of total binding is plotted
against the cumulative fraction of kinases (a Lorenz curve). The Gini
coefficient, which measures how unequally binding is distributed across the kinome,
is twice the area between this curve and the diagonal of equal
distribution.
The original work computes it from percent-inhibition values. As with the
entropy, we computed it on the baseline-subtracted log-affinity profile, using
the standard discrete formula:
\begin{equation}
  G(\beta) = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N} i \cdot x_{(i)}}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{(i)}} - \frac{N+1}{N},
  \qquad x_{(i)} = \max(\mathrm{p}K_{d,i} - \beta,\, 0)\  \text{sorted ascending}.
\end{equation}
We swept $\beta$ over the same range as for the entropy.
A compound whose binding is concentrated on one kinase has a highly unequal
distribution ($G$ is near 1), while a compound that binds many kinases equally has
$G$ near 0. Higher values therefore indicate greater selectivity.

\paragraph{Ratio.}
Classical pharmacological selectivity is pairwise. It quantifies how much more tightly a
compound binds its primary target than an off-target.
Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} also reported a score of this kind alongside
the S-score: the fraction of tested kinases bound within tenfold of the
affinity for the compound's intended primary target, which combines a ratio
cutoff with an S-score-style count. Anchoring on an annotated primary target
avoids the ratio's near-tie instability (Type~2, Results). The score, however,
has its own unstandardized parameter (the tenfold cutoff) and, as a fraction of
the panel, violates D3 in the same way as the S-score.
We instead implemented the pairwise comparison from the binding profile alone,
as every measure compared here is a function of the profile (Motivated
Properties, below). We included the reference off-target as an explicit parameter
whose effect can be studied directly: the fold-selectivity, in log units,
between the primary target and the $k$-th ranked off-target:
\begin{equation}
  R(k) = \mathrm{p}K_{d,(1)} - \mathrm{p}K_{d,(k+1)}
       = \log_{10}\frac{K_{d,(k+1)}}{K_{d,(1)}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{p}K_{d,(j)}$ is the $j$-th largest affinity in the profile and
$k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ selects the reference off-target. We capped $k$ at 5
because, on the sparser Klaeger panel, a large share of compounds already have
fewer than five active kinases (median 4; $51\%$ have fewer than five). For
those compounds, a larger $k$ would compare the primary target against the
assay floor instead of an off-target. Bosc et al.'s ranking
score\cite{Bosc2017} is the same difference taken at a chosen rank, so $R(k)$
is their ranking score at rank $k + 1$. On
Anastassiadis, $R(k)$ is a difference in percent inhibition rather than a
log fold-selectivity.

Our $R(k)$ scores every compound, substituting the value assigned to
non-detection for a missing off-target. Bosc et al.'s ranking score also
scores every compound, on a matrix with non-binding pairs set to $10.1~\mu$M,
so $R(k)$ shares its D1 violation with the published score.

The concentration- and target-dependent selectivity (CATDS) score of Klaeger
et al.\cite{Klaeger2017} is not a ratio in this sense, since it normalizes over
the whole panel (Background). A proxy computed from our profiles correlates
more with the Gini than with $R(k)$ on every dataset (Discussion, Supporting
Information Table~S2), but it does not reproduce the concentration dependence of the
published score. We therefore represent the ratio family by $R(k)$ alone.

\paragraph{Comparators.}
Three further measures are used in the property and recovery analyses. The
active count is the number of targets above the activity threshold $\tau^*$
(below), negated so that higher values are more selective. The second is the
entropy with its baseline at the activity threshold, $\beta = \tau^*$, which
gives no weight to binding below $\tau^*$. The third is the S-score with its
threshold at the floor $x_0$ (below), the share of the panel with detected
binding. All three are scored only for compounds with a target above $\tau^*$,
as D1 (below) requires.
% \emph{In words:} how big is the affinity gap between the primary target and the
% next-best one? A larger gap means greater selectivity.

\paragraph{Faithfulness to the original definitions.}
The S-score and the ratio are computed in the form given by their original
sources (a panel-hit fraction and a primary-to-off-target affinity ratio,
respectively).
For the entropy and the Gini coefficient, we used the baseline-subtracted
log-affinity profile in place of the original association constants (entropy)
and percent-inhibition values (Gini), so that all four metrics operate on one
common scale. The baseline $\beta$ is therefore a parameter of our
operationalization, not of the published definitions. Unless stated otherwise,
every entropy and Gini result in this paper concerns this form.

To test how much this choice matters, we also computed the entropy in its
published form, with association-constant weighting
($p_i = K_{a,i}/\sum_j K_{a,j}$), and the Gini of the association constants as
an affinity-scale analogue. Two choices are involved. The first is the
weighting. The second is the treatment of pairs coded at the floor: pairs with
no detected binding on Davis and Klaeger, and pairs not tested on Metz. The
log-affinity forms with $\beta = x_0$ give these pairs no weight. Association
constants give them a small positive weight if they are kept at the floor
value. We report both treatments, floor-coded pairs kept at the floor and given
no weight. Neither is free of assumptions, since a floor-coded pair has no
measured affinity. On Anastassiadis, the one dataset with a native
percent-inhibition scale, we also computed the Gini without baseline
subtraction. Results compares these forms with the log-affinity forms
(Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}; Supporting Information, Table~S3).

\subsection{Rank Stability Analysis}
\label{sec:methods:stability}

For each definition and each parameter value, compounds were ranked from most
selective (rank 1) to least selective (rank $n$, where $n$ is the number of
compounds in the dataset). The S-score and the raw Shannon
entropy both run opposite to this convention (a higher fraction of panel hits,
or a higher raw entropy, indicates a \emph{more} promiscuous compound), so
both are negated before ranking throughout this paper. The Gini coefficient
and $R(k)$ already increase with selectivity and are used unnegated.
Ties were broken by average rank.
Where the entropy and Gini are undefined (no kinase above the baseline
$\beta$), our implementation sets both scores to $0$. This ranks the compound as maximally selective
under the (negated) entropy measure, tied with single-target profiles, and as minimally
selective under the Gini. A compound can be undefined at some baselines of the
sweep and defined at others. Results compares this with computing instability
only over the configurations in which a compound's score is defined. The ratio substitutes the value assigned to non-detection for a
missing off-target (above): $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ on Davis and Klaeger,
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$ on Metz, and $0\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis. The
S-score is defined for every compound.
This yielded 30 rank vectors per dataset for Davis, Klaeger, and Metz (11 for
the S-score, 7 for entropy, 7 for Gini, and 5 for ratio), and 28 for
Anastassiadis, whose coarser native percent-inhibition scale supports only 9
S-score threshold values rather than 11.

For each compound, rank instability was quantified as the standard deviation
of its rank across all configurations for that dataset (overall instability)
and separately
across parameter configurations within each definition family (within-family
instability).
Cross-definition-family agreement was assessed using Spearman rank correlation
between the median rank vectors of each definition family.
The median across parameter values was used because it is less sensitive to
outlying configurations than the mean.
Using mean ranks instead changes no correlation by more than $0.08$ on any
dataset.

Associations between rank instability and binding profile features were
assessed using Spearman correlation on Klaeger and Davis. Both report
$\mathrm{p}K_d$, so the features are on the same scale and each relation found
on one can be tested on the other.
Profile features computed for each compound included: the number of active
kinases ($\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$), the gap between the top and second-ranked
affinities, the standard deviation and range of active affinities, the
absolute affinity of the second-ranked target, and the skewness of the active
affinities. The first two were also tested on Anastassiadis and Metz, against
entropy and ratio instability respectively (Results).
These associations are exploratory. Five further features were examined
(Results), and correction for multiple testing covers only the 24 tests of
Table~\ref{tab:predictors}.
All correlations are reported with two-tailed $p$-values, uncorrected for
multiple testing unless stated. Significance
thresholds of $p < 0.05$, $p < 0.01$, and $p < 0.001$ are denoted *, **, and
*** respectively.
Analyses were performed in Python 3 using NumPy, pandas, and
SciPy.\cite{NumPy,pandas,SciPy}
A secondary analysis relating selectivity rankings to clinical adverse-event
reports is summarized in the Limitations section.
The analysis code and full per-definition results are available in the code
repository (see Data and Software Availability), and Supporting Information
Table~S7 lists the script that computes each reported result.

\subsection{Motivated Properties}
\label{sec:methods:properties}

The instability patterns reported in Results motivate three properties. Each is
stated formally below, together with the failure mode it targets and how a
measure is assessed against it. We evaluate the four definitions, the two
comparators and the candidate measure against them (Results).
Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_N) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ denote a compound's
binding profile across $N$ kinases, where $x_i = \mathrm{p}K_{d,i}$ and $N$ is
the panel size, which varies across datasets and subpanels. A selectivity measure is a
function $f : \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}$, defined for every panel size $N$,
where higher values indicate greater selectivity. A ranking over a set of
compounds is defined by ordering their $f$-scores. We write $x_0$ for the
assay floor ($\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ on Davis and Klaeger,
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$ on Metz, and $20\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis, where
$54\%$ of entries lie between $0\%$ and $20\%$, so a floor at $0\%$ would admit
assay noise) and $\tau^*$ for the activity threshold
($\mathrm{p}K_d$ or $\mathrm{p}K_i = 6$; $50\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis).

\textbf{D1 (reliability threshold).} $f(\mathbf{x})$ is undefined, or
explicitly flagged as unreliable, whenever $\max_i x_i \leq \tau^*$.

A compound with no binding above the activity threshold has no target against
which to judge selectivity. Its rank then depends on how
each measure handles such profiles (Type~1, Results). D1 is a
reporting rule and is assessed as such.

\textbf{D2 (distributional consistency).} Let $B = [x_0, \tau^*]$, the interval
between the floor and the activity threshold, where an
analyst must decide how much sub-threshold binding counts. Let $\theta$ be a parameter
of $f$ that an analyst must choose on the affinity scale (the S-score's $\tau$
and the entropy's and Gini's $\beta$, which place the activity boundary, or the
level below which the ratio replaces an off-target affinity). A pair of compounds $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$
\emph{reverses in $B$} if $f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) - f(\mathbf{y}; \theta)$ is
positive at some $\theta \in B$ and negative at another. D2 requires that at
most a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the pairs of compounds that D1 allows to be
scored reverse in $B$.

We use $\varepsilon = 1\%$. D2 addresses the baseline sensitivity of compounds
with few active kinases (Results) and is assessed empirically on each dataset.
The S-score is evaluated at every observed affinity in $B$, where its value
changes, and the other parameters on 201 equally spaced values (101 values
change no fraction by more than $0.1$ percentage point). Fixing a parameter
before analysis is a protocol decision. It does not make a ranking robust to
the parameter, and D2 reports how much the ranking would change had a
different value been chosen.

\textbf{D3 (monotonicity under weak off-target addition).} For any $\mathbf{x}$
with $\max_i x_i > \tau^*$, if $\mathbf{x}' = (x_1, \ldots, x_N, x_{N+1})$ with
$x_0 < x_{N+1} \leq \tau^*$, then $f(\mathbf{x}') \leq f(\mathbf{x})$.

That is, adding a kinase that the compound binds above the floor but below the
activity threshold must not increase its score. Otherwise, a compound can be
made to look more selective by profiling it against more kinases it barely
binds. D3 is assessed from each measure's functional form.

We state no property for the ratio's choice of reference off-target. Changing
which kinases count as tolerated co-targets changes the question a selectivity
score answers, so scores for different references need not agree. The ratio's
dependence on $k$ is reported as an empirical result (Type~2, Results).

\textbf{Recoverability.} Separately from the properties, we evaluate how well
each measure's ranking is recovered from smaller panels. Subpanels of $m$
targets are drawn uniformly at random from the curated targets. Two compounds
are clearly separated if their full-panel ranks are at least a quarter of the
scored compounds apart. Recoverability at size $m$ is the share of clearly
separated pairs that are scored on the subpanel and keep their order, with a
pair tied on the subpanel counted as one half. It therefore combines ordering with coverage, the share of compounds still scored,
and we also report each separately. Recoverability is a criterion for this
sampling design, not a property that every profile can meet: a subpanel that
omits all of a compound's active targets cannot recover its rank.

\subsection{A Candidate Measure}
\label{sec:methods:candidate}

The candidate measure is an entropy-based score that meets D1 and D3 and
weights binding between the floor and the activity threshold continuously in
affinity. The active count and the entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ meet D3 by
giving such binding no weight, and the S-score with $\tau = x_0$ by counting
every kinase above the floor equally. For a profile
$\mathbf{x}$ with $\max_i x_i > \tau^*$ (undefined otherwise, satisfying D1),
define
\begin{equation}
  w_i = \mathbf{1}[x_i > x_0]\; T \log\!\big(1 + e^{(x_i - \beta)/T}\big), \qquad
  p_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i,
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is the assay floor, $\beta$ is a smooth emphasis baseline (set to
$x_0$ in the results below), and $T$ is a smoothing width in the native
units of the profile. We set $T = \tau^* - x_0$, the distance from the floor
to the activity threshold: $1$ $\mathrm{p}K_d$ unit on Davis and Klaeger,
$2$ $\mathrm{p}K_i$ units on Metz, and $30$ percentage points on
Anastassiadis, whose native scale is $0$--$100\%$ inhibition rather than
log-affinity. The function $f$ is the negative Shannon entropy of the
smoothed, normalized profile, i.e.\ the negative logarithm of the
effective number of targets the compound binds.

The candidate differs from the selectivity entropy in two ways. First, it is
undefined for a compound with no active target, as D1 requires. Second, it
replaces the entropy's hard cutoff $\max(x_i - \beta, 0)$ with a softplus, for
D3. The softplus is applied only to kinases above the floor $x_0$, since it
would otherwise give weight to non-binders. The hard cutoff gives a kinase just
above $\beta$ a weight close to zero, and this is what lets a sub-threshold
addition raise the entropy's score. The softplus gives
every gated kinase a weight of at least $T \ln 2$, which rules this out:

\textbf{Proposition.} With $\beta = x_0$ and
$T \geq (\tau^* - x_0)/\ln(2^e - 1) \approx 0.58\,(\tau^* - x_0)$, adding a
kinase with affinity in $(x_0, \tau^*]$ never raises the candidate's score.

\textit{Proof.} Every gated kinase, the added one included, has weight
above $m = T\ln 2$. Since the softplus increases with affinity, an added kinase
with affinity at most $\tau^*$ has weight
$a \leq T\ln(1 + e^{(\tau^* - x_0)/T})$, and, by the assumption on $T$
in the proposition, this is at
most $e\,m$.

Let the original profile have $n \geq 1$ gated kinases ($n \geq 1$ because D1
scores it only if some kinase lies above $\tau^* > x_0$), total weight
$W > nm$, and entropy $H$ (in nats), and let $q = a/(W + a)$ be the share of
the total weight carried by the added kinase. Once that kinase is added, the
entropy is $(1 - q)H + h(q)$, where $h(q) = -q\ln q - (1 - q)\ln(1 - q)$ is the
binary entropy. So, the entropy does not decrease if and only if
$H \leq h(q)/q$. Because $h$ is concave with
$h(0) = 0$, $h(q)/q$ decreases in $q$. Since $q < r/(n + r)$ with
$r = a/m \leq e$, this gives
$h(q)/q > \ln\frac{n + r}{r} + \frac{n}{r}\ln\frac{n + r}{n}$. As $H \leq \ln n$,
it suffices that $\ln n$ is at most this bound, which rearranges to
$\ln r \leq (1 + \frac{n}{r})\ln(1 + \frac{r}{n})$. The right-hand side is at
least $1$ for all $n, r > 0$, because $\ln(1 + t) \geq t/(1 + t)$, and
$\ln r \leq 1$. The entropy therefore does not decrease, so the score (its
negative) does not increase. $\square$

The choice $T = \tau^* - x_0$ satisfies this condition on every dataset. With
this $T$, the same argument covers every $\beta \in B$: the lightest gated
kinase then has weight above $m = T\ln(1 + e^{(x_0 - \beta)/T})$, and
$a/m \leq 2.22 < e$.

The gate makes the candidate discontinuous at $x_0$: a kinase just above the
floor carries weight $T \ln 2$, and one at the floor carries none. The D3
guarantee depends on this minimum weight, not on the smoothness of the hinge.
Measurement noise of $0.3$ p$K$ units ($9$ percentage points on Anastassiadis)
changes which compounds D1 scores for at most 11 compounds in any draw. Over
the compounds scored with and without noise, the candidate's ranking stays at
Spearman $0.978$ or higher with the noise-free ranking, against $0.983$ or
higher for the entropy with $\beta = x_0$.
