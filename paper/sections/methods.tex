\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Datasets, Target Selection and Missing Values}
\label{sec:methods:data}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the four datasets. The Davis
dataset\cite{Davis2011} reports dissociation constants from a
competition-binding assay, and the Klaeger dataset\cite{Klaeger2017} apparent
dissociation constants from Kinobeads chemoproteomics in cell lysate (beads
carrying kinase inhibitors that capture kinases from the lysate, with a
mass-spectrometry readout). Both were
converted, as in DeepDTA\cite{DeepDTA}, to
$\mathrm{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d / 10^9)$. The
Anastassiadis dataset\cite{Anastassiadis2011} reports residual kinase activity
in a radiometric assay at $0.5~\mu$M inhibitor, which we converted to percent
inhibition ($100$ minus residual activity, clipped to $[0, 100]$). The Metz
dataset\cite{Metz2011} reports $\mathrm{p}K_i$ values from a
medicinal-chemistry campaign. Because the assays and readouts differ, each
score is computed on its dataset's own readout, and datasets are compared only
through the rankings computed within each.

\paragraph{Target selection.} Selectivity across kinases is expressed as a
single value, so each kinase should be counted once. A panel that lists several
constructs (recombinant forms of a protein, such as mutants or single domains)
of one gene lists that kinase several times. The released Davis
panel lists 433 constructs. They include mutant, phosphorylation-state
and single-domain constructs of the same gene (12 for EGFR), 20 lipid kinases
and three non-human kinases, and for 26 of the 68 compounds the two best-bound
constructs are forms of one gene. We keep one reference construct per human
protein-kinase gene: the wild-type construct, or its catalytic or N-terminal
kinase domain where several domains are profiled. Genes profiled only as
mutants are removed. The 343 Klaeger targets are all proteins that the beads
captured and mass spectrometry measured, including complex subunits, such as
cyclins, that bind the captured kinases. We keep the 217 single-gene protein kinases that Klaeger et
al.\ annotate, except Q6ZSR9 (which UniProt lists as an uncharacterized protein
without a kinase domain). This removes lipid kinases, four protein groups, and
BCR and TRRAP, which the source does not annotate as kinases and flags as
possibly co-enriched with ABL1 and CDK8. On Anastassiadis we keep the
first-listed of nine kinases profiled in two complexes or isoforms. The Metz
columns are already one human protein kinase each. We call the targets kept on
each dataset its \emph{curated panel} (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). The code repository lists
every target with the reason it was kept or removed, and Supporting
Information (Section~S1) compares the released panels.

\paragraph{Floor-coded and missing values.} On Davis, Klaeger and Metz, several
kinds of pairs receive the same floor value $x_0$, which is not a measured
detection limit. On Davis, the
released matrix assigns $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ ($K_d = 10~\mu$M) to pairs with
no detected binding, following DeepDTA\cite{DeepDTA}; Bosc et
al.\cite{Bosc2017} used nearly the same value ($10.1~\mu$M). On Klaeger, we kept the 5{,}123
drug--target pairs that the original authors classified as high confidence. We
coded at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ three kinds of pairs: those with no detected
binding, those whose fit was classified as low confidence, and the 194 measured
affinities weaker than $10~\mu$M. A low-confidence fit does not establish weak
binding: of the 405 such pairs on curated targets, 375 have a fitted
$\mathrm{p}K_d$ above 5. Giving all of these pairs the floor value is an
analysis convention. On Metz, pairs that were not tested are coded at
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$, the lowest value reported in the data. A median Metz
compound is measured against only 21 of the 172 kinases, so we kept the 704
compounds measured against at least 50 (median 64). On Anastassiadis, the
$1.1\%$ of missing entries were set to $0\%$ inhibition. Most pairs on this
panel show little inhibition: $85\%$ of the entries are at or below $20\%$,
and each $5\%$ interval above $20\%$ holds less than $3\%$ of the entries. We
place $x_0$ at $20\%$, the upper end of these low values, as an operational
cutoff for weak inhibition.

A compound is \emph{active} against a target above the activity threshold $\tau^*$:
$\mathrm{p}K_d$ or $\mathrm{p}K_i$ $6$ ($K_d$ or $K_i$ of $1~\mu$M) on the
affinity datasets, and $50\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis. With a Hill
coefficient of 1, $50\%$ inhibition at $0.5~\mu$M inhibitor means an IC$_{50}$
of $0.5~\mu$M, close to the $1~\mu$M of the other datasets. Supporting
Information (Section~S1) repeats the Davis comparison of the scores with the
threshold one log unit higher. An \emph{active compound} has at least one
target above $\tau^*$.

\subsection{Selectivity Scores and Their Implementations}
\label{sec:methods:defs}

A compound's profile is the vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_N)$ of its
readouts across the $N$ panel targets, with higher values for tighter binding
or stronger inhibition. Every score is oriented so that higher values are more
selective (Table~\ref{tab:implementations}).

\paragraph{S-score.} The S-score of Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} is the fraction of the panel
bound above a threshold,
\begin{equation}
  S(\tau) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[x_i > \tau],
\end{equation}
negated. They reported $K_d$ cutoffs of $3~\mu$M and $100$~nM, which lie
within our sweep of $\tau$.

\paragraph{Selectivity entropy.} The selectivity entropy of Uitdehaag and
Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} is the Shannon entropy
$H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i$ (in bits) of the profile normalized to a distribution,
negated. In its published form, $p_i = K_{a,i} / \sum_j K_{a,j}$, with
association constants $K_{a,i} = 1/K_{d,i}$ ($1/K_{i,i}$ on Metz). It needs
affinity constants, so it is not computed on Anastassiadis. To place all four
definitions on
one scale, we also use a log-affinity form (on Anastassiadis, percent
inhibition), which weights each target by its readout above a baseline
$\beta$:
\begin{equation}
  p_i = \frac{\max(x_i - \beta,\, 0)}{\sum_j \max(x_j - \beta,\, 0)}.
\end{equation}
The baseline is a parameter we introduce; it has no published reference
values.

\paragraph{Gini coefficient.} Graczyk's Gini coefficient\cite{Graczyk2010} measures how unequally
binding is spread over the panel. Its published form uses percent-inhibition
values. We compute it on the same baseline-subtracted weights,
\begin{equation}
  G(\beta) = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N} i \cdot w_{(i)}}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{(i)}} - \frac{N+1}{N},
  \qquad w_{(i)} = \max(x_i - \beta,\, 0)\ \text{sorted ascending},
\end{equation}
and on Anastassiadis also in its native form. 

Unless stated otherwise, both the
entropy and Gini are in their log-affinity forms.

\paragraph{Ratio.} The ratio compares the primary target with the $k$-th
ranked off-target,
\begin{equation}
  R_b(k) = x_{(1)} - \max(x_{(k+1)}, b),
\end{equation}
where $x_{(j)}$ is the $j$-th largest value. The level $b$ is the lowest coded
value ($5.0$ on Davis and Klaeger, $4.0$ on Metz and $0\%$ on Anastassiadis),
except in the recovery analysis, where it is $x_0$, and in D2, where it is
swept. At this level $R_b(k)$ equals the ranking score of Bosc
et al.\cite{Bosc2017} at rank $k + 1$. We cap $k$ at 5 because half of the Klaeger compounds
have fewer than five active kinases.

\paragraph{Partition index.} The partition index of Cheng et al.\cite{Cheng2010} is the fraction of a
compound's molecules that would bind a reference target in a mixture of all panel kinases,
$K_{a,\mathrm{ref}} / \sum_j K_{a,j}$. Only Klaeger names an intended target
for each compound, so we use the most strongly bound target as the reference
($P_{\max}$), as Uitdehaag and Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} did. Like the
published entropy, it is not computed on Anastassiadis. The CATDS score
of Klaeger et al.\ is the same kind of fraction, for a designated target at one
concentration. It needs Kinobeads dose--response curves, which the other three
datasets lack, so it is not compared across datasets (Supporting Information,
Section~S6, compares a proxy).

\paragraph{Conventions.} Where the entropy and Gini are undefined, we set both
to $0$. This ranks the compound as maximally selective under the entropy and
minimally selective under the Gini. $R_b(k)$ uses $b$ for a missing
off-target and so scores every compound, as Bosc et al.'s ranking score does.
Log-affinity weights with $\beta = x_0$ give floor-coded pairs zero weight.
Association constants give them a small positive weight if they are kept at the
floor value. For the published entropy and $P_{\max}$, we give them zero weight.
As a sensitivity analysis for the published entropy, we keep floor-coded
pairs at the floor value. On Klaeger, we also give every measured
high-confidence affinity its measured value, including the 194 weaker than
the floor, and the other floor-coded pairs zero weight. 
No empirical data supports either assigning zero-weight or $x_0$ value
to floor-coded pairs.

\paragraph{Comparators.} The comparators are simple variants of existing
scores. The only new score in this study is the gated
entropy. The active count (the number of targets above $\tau^*$, which is the
numerator of the S-score at $\tau^*$, with fewer more selective), the entropy
with $\beta = \tau^*$
and the S-score with $\tau = x_0$ (the share of the panel with detected
binding) are scored only for active compounds. Otherwise, a compound with no
active target would have an active count of 0, fewer than any active compound,
and would rank as the most selective.

\subsection{Ranking Comparisons and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:methods:stability}

For each score and parameter value, compounds are ranked from most selective
(rank 1), with ties given their average rank. Ranks are always computed among
a stated set of compounds. Analyses of compounds with an active kinase rank
them among themselves. Analyses that compare
compounds with and without an active kinase rank all compounds. Agreement
between two definitions is taken to be the Spearman correlation between their median rank
vectors over each definition's parameter sweep: $\tau$ in steps of $0.25$
(11 values) and $\beta$ in steps of $0.25$ (7 values) on the $\mathrm{p}K$
datasets, $\tau$ in steps of $5\%$ (9 values) and $\beta$ in steps of $5\%$
(7 values) on Anastassiadis, and $k = 1, \ldots, 5$. Using mean ranks instead
changes the correlations among all compounds by at most $0.08$. Bootstrap
$95\%$ intervals use 1{,}000 resamples of compounds.

Each parameter value gives one ranking of the compounds. A compound's
\emph{rank instability} is the standard deviation of its ranks across one
definition's sweep. Its \emph{aggregated rank instability} covers all four
definitions. Aggregation is done by taking the standard deviation of the
compound's ranks over the rankings from all four sweeps together. Each
definition therefore counts in proportion to its number of parameter values,
and the S-score, with the most values, carries the most weight.
We compute the Spearman correlation between within-definition instability and
each profile feature: the number of active kinases, the top$_1$--top$_2$
affinity gap, the second-ranked affinity, and the standard deviation, range
and skewness of the active affinities. A feature undefined for a compound with
too few active kinases is set to $0$. Supporting Information (Section~S3)
repeats the analysis with these compounds excluded.
These associations are exploratory. A Bonferroni correction, checked against
the Holm and Benjamini--Hochberg procedures, is applied only to the 24 tests per
dataset in Supporting Information Table~S4 (six features and four
definitions), while other $p$-values are uncorrected.

To compare how much rankings change between datasets with how much they
change between definitions, we matched 46 compounds profiled in both Davis and
Klaeger by PubChem CID and restricted both panels to the 201 kinases they share
(matched by gene symbol, with renamed symbols mapped to current ones). We then
computed the Spearman correlation between each definition's median ranking on
Davis and on Klaeger, and between each pair of definitions' median rankings
within each dataset.

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria and the Gated Entropy}
\label{sec:methods:criteria}

A selectivity measure $f$ is a function that, for a given profile $\mathbf{x}$
of any length $N$, returns a real number $f(\mathbf{x})$, with higher values
for more selective profiles. Let $B = [x_0, \tau^*]$ be the interval between
the floor and the activity threshold. We present four criteria. D1 decides
which compounds are scored, D2 and D3 concern how their scores change when the
activity boundary moves and when the panel grows, and D4 concerns the direction
of the score.

\textbf{D1 (activity eligibility).} $f(\mathbf{x})$ is undefined, or flagged as
not eligible, whenever $\max_i x_i \leq \tau^*$. A compound with no target
above $\tau^*$ has no target against which to judge selectivity. D1 is a
reporting rule, so it is judged by construction: a score satisfies it if its
definition withholds or flags these compounds. Bosc et
al.\cite{Bosc2017} withheld ranks for such compounds under the S-score only,
whereas D1 applies the rule to every score.

\textbf{D2 (distributional consistency).} Let $\theta$ be the parameter that
places a score's activity boundary, i.e. the S-score's $\tau$, the entropy's and
Gini's $\beta$, the gated entropy's gate $x_0$ (with $\beta$ moved with it and
$T$ fixed), and the ratio's level $b$. A
pair of compounds \emph{reverses in $B$} if $f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) -
f(\mathbf{y}; \theta)$ is positive at some $\theta \in B$ and negative at
another. D2 requires that at most $\varepsilon = 1\%$ of the pairs of compounds
scored under D1 reverse in $B$, on every dataset and, for the ratio, for every
$k$. D2 is therefore judged empirically, on the four datasets. The tolerance $\varepsilon$ is our choice (Results report the range of
$\varepsilon$ over which the verdicts stay the same). To count reversals, each
score is evaluated at a set of values of $\theta$ in $B$. The S-score changes
only where $\tau$ passes an observed value, so it is evaluated at every observed
value in $B$ and its percentages are exact. The gated entropy jumps where its
gate passes an observed value, so the gate is evaluated at each observed value
in $B$, just below each, and at 201 equally spaced values. The other parameters
are evaluated at the 201 values. Apart from the S-score's, the percentages are
therefore estimates.

Some scores fix the boundary at one value, e.g. the active count, which
counts the targets above $\tau^*$. Such a score is assessed by sweeping that
value over $B$, so that a fixed value does not let a score pass D2 vacuously.
The value of $\theta$ is set in the analysis protocol according to the intended
use, e.g. by whether binding weaker than $\tau^*$ matters at the
concentration at which the compound will be used in practice. The resulting rankings are
sensitive to this choice, and D2 reports this sensitivity as a diagnostic.
At different values of $\theta$, a score
can measure different things: on Davis, the S-score at $\tau = 5$ counts the
targets with $K_d$ below $10~\mu$M, and at $\tau = 6$ those below $1~\mu$M.
Its rankings can reasonably differ between the two.

\textbf{D3 (monotonicity under weak-target addition).} For any $\mathbf{x}$ with
$\max_i x_i > \tau^*$, if $\mathbf{x}' = (x_1, \ldots, x_N, x_{N+1})$ with
$x_0 < x_{N+1} \leq \tau^*$, then $f(\mathbf{x}') \leq f(\mathbf{x})$. Otherwise a
compound can be made to look more selective by profiling it against more
kinases it binds weakly. D3 is judged by construction: a score
satisfies it if adding such a target can be proved never to raise the score,
and violates it if a profile can be constructed on which it does. Tests on
measured profiles (Results) are secondary checks of D3.

\textbf{D4 (orientation).} Fix the panel size $N \geq 2$ and all of the
score's parameters. For every value $a$ above $\tau^*$ and above every cutoff
and baseline the score uses, let $\mathbf{v}_m$ be the profile with $m$ targets
at $a$ and the other $N - m$ at the floor, for $m = 1, \ldots, N$. D4 requires
$f(\mathbf{v}_{m+1}) \leq f(\mathbf{v}_m)$ for $m = 1, \ldots, N - 1$, and
$f(\mathbf{v}_1) > f(\mathbf{v}_N)$. These two conditions define what we take as
the correct direction of a score: on the same panel, binding equally strongly
to more targets must not make a compound look more selective, and binding to
one target must be more selective than binding equally strongly to all. Strict
decrease at every step is not required: $R_b(k)$ with $1 \leq k < N$ equals
$a - \max(x_0, b)$ for $m \leq k$ and $0$ for $m > k$. D4 is judged by
construction.

When floor-coded pairs have zero weight, the profiles $\mathbf{v}_m$ become
both broader and more evenly weighted as $m$ increases. When they keep a
positive weight, they form a background of small weights, and turning them
into equally strong targets moves weight from this background into a few large
weights. The distribution can then become less even and the score rise, so a
score can fail D4 because of its floor treatment, and each implementation is
assessed with its own (example in Supporting Information, Section~S4).

D4 constrains only the profiles $\mathbf{v}_m$. On a profile with unequal
affinities, adding an active target that binds much more strongly than the
others moves most of the weight to it, and a score that measures how
concentrated binding is, such as the entropy or $P_{\max}$, then rates the
compound as more selective (Supporting Information, Section~S4). Whether that
rating is correct depends on whether selectivity is taken to mean breadth or
concentration, which D4 leaves open.

We state no criterion for the ratio's choice of reference off-target, because
different references answer different questions.

\paragraph{The gated entropy.} For a profile with $\max_i x_i > \tau^*$
(undefined otherwise, as D1 requires), define
\begin{equation}
  w_i = \mathbf{1}[x_i > x_0]\; T \log\!\big(1 + e^{(x_i - \beta)/T}\big), \qquad
  p_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i,
  \label{eq:candidate}
\end{equation}
with the emphasis baseline $\beta = x_0$ and the smoothing width
$T = \tau^* - x_0$ ($1$ $\mathrm{p}K$ unit on Davis and
Klaeger, $2$ on Metz, and $30$ percentage points on Anastassiadis). It is the
negative Shannon entropy of a gated, smoothed profile. The non-binder gate
$\mathbf{1}[x_i > x_0]$ gives floor-coded pairs zero weight. The softplus
replaces the entropy's hard cutoff $\max(x_i - \beta, 0)$, which gives a
kinase just above $\beta$ a weight close to zero.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (monotonicity).} With $\beta = x_0$, adding a kinase with a value in
$(x_0, \tau^*]$ never raises $f$ if and only if
$T \geq (\tau^* - x_0)/\ln(2^e - 1) \approx 0.58\,(\tau^* - x_0)$. With
$T = \tau^* - x_0$, it never raises $f$ for any $\beta \in B$.

The proof of Proposition 1 (Supporting Information, Section~S4) has two parts. For the
\emph{if} part, every gated target has weight above $m = T\ln 2$, and the added
target has weight at most $e\,m$. This bound on its share of the total weight
is enough for the entropy not to decrease. For the \emph{only if} part, for
any smaller $T$, a profile with one strong target and many kinases just above
the floor scores higher after a kinase is added at $\tau^*$. For $\beta \neq x_0$
only the \emph{if} direction is known.

Because a target at the floor has weight $0$ and any target above it has weight
above $m = T\ln 2$, the score jumps when a target's value crosses $x_0$.
For example, on Davis or Klaeger ($x_0 = 5$, $T = 1$), take a profile with
one active target at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 7$. Adding a second target at the floor
leaves the score at $0$, and adding one just above the floor lowers it by
$0.80$ bits. Measurement noise near the floor could therefore change scores.
To measure this effect, we perturbed the measured values
above the floor (normal noise with standard deviation $0.1$ and $0.3$
$\mathrm{p}K$ units), set values pushed below the floor to the floor, and left
floor-coded pairs fixed. On Anastassiadis, every entry was perturbed, with
standard deviation $3$ and $9$ percentage points. Each setting used 50 draws.
Noise can also change which compounds are scored: a compound whose strongest
target lies just above $\tau^*$ can fall below it, and the reverse. D1 was
therefore applied to each noisy profile, and rankings were compared over the
compounds scored both with and without noise.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (uniqueness for fixed weights).} Fix the weights, that
is, $x_0$, $\beta$ and $T$ in Equation~(\ref{eq:candidate}), and let
$\mathbf{p}$ be the shares of a profile's positive gated weights. Suppose a
score has the form $-F(\mathbf{p})$, where $F$ is continuous, increases with $n$ when all $n$ shares are equal, and has Shannon's
grouping property\cite{Shannon1948}: when the kinases are split into groups,
$F$ equals its value across the groups plus the share-weighted values within
them. Then the score is a positive multiple of the gated entropy and ranks
compounds as the gated entropy does.

The proof applies Shannon's uniqueness theorem to the weight shares
(Supporting Information, Section~S4). R\'enyi entropies of order other than 1
lack the grouping property, so they are excluded.

Let us call shares $\mathbf{q}$ \emph{more even} than shares $\mathbf{p}$ on the same
panel if, for every $j$, the $j$ largest entries of $\mathbf{q}$ sum to no more
than the $j$ largest entries of $\mathbf{p}$.

\textbf{Proposition 3 (broadening).} On a fixed panel, let a profile have
positive weights $w_1, \ldots, w_r$ ($r \geq 1$) and zero weights elsewhere,
and let a score be a function $f$ of the profile's weight shares with
$f(\mathbf{q}) \leq f(\mathbf{p})$ whenever $\mathbf{q}$ is more even than
$\mathbf{p}$. Changing one zero weight to $a$ with $0 < a \leq \min_i w_i$
does not raise $f$.

The proof (Supporting Information, Section~S4) shows that the added weight,
being the smallest, makes the new shares more even than the old ones. The
negative Shannon and R\'enyi entropies, the largest share and the Gini
coefficient all have this property. For a score whose
weight increases with affinity, such as the entropy, the gated entropy,
$P_{\max}$ and the Gini, Proposition 3 therefore means that turning a
floor-coded pair into a target bound no more strongly than the weakest
weighted target never makes the compound look more selective. The condition on $a$ is needed: adding a target
tied with the strongest can lower the entropy.


\subsection{Recovery from Smaller Panels}
\label{sec:methods:recovery}

Profiling panels range from tens to hundreds of kinases, so a score computed on
a smaller panel is only useful if it ranks compounds as it would on a larger
one. We therefore measure how much of each score's full-panel ranking random
smaller panels retain, and for how many compounds.

Subpanels of $m$ targets were drawn uniformly at random from the curated
targets, 50 per size, at $m = 10$, $20$, $30$, $40$, $50$, $65$, $80$, $100$,
$125$, $150$, $200$, $250$ and $300$ below the full panel. Every score in
Table~\ref{tab:implementations}, including the comparators, was computed on the
same subpanels, with $\tau = \tau^*$ for the S-score, $\beta = x_0$ for the
entropy and Gini, and $k = 1$ and $b = x_0$ for the ratio. D1 was applied to every score on every
subpanel. \emph{Coverage} is the share of compounds scored on the full panel that are
still scored on a subpanel, averaged over the 50 subpanels of each size. It
varies between subpanels, but on any one subpanel it is the same for every
score as every score uses the same D1 rule (a compound is scored under all
scores or none). 

\emph{Recoverability}
is the share of clearly separated full-panel pairs (ranks at least a quarter
of the scored compounds apart) that are scored on the subpanel in the same
order, with a pair tied on the subpanel counted as one half. \emph{Conditional
agreement} is the Spearman correlation between subpanel and full-panel scores
over the compounds scored on both. Each score is compared with its own
full-panel ranking, then, its clearly separated pairs are chosen from that
ranking. Thus, recoverability measures how much of a score's own ordering a subpanel
keeps. We write $p^*$ for the smallest tested
size at which the mean conditional agreement over subpanels exceeds $0.90$, a
descriptive benchmark that we chose. To test whether
compounds whose score depends heavily on one target are harder to recover, we
compute two quantities for each compound: its rank error at 50 targets, the
mean absolute change in its rank (as a share of the compounds) over the
subpanels on which it is scored, and the largest change in its full-panel score
when one target is left out. We report the Spearman correlation between them
over the scored compounds. As published, none of the four definitions has an
