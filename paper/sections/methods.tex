\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Datasets, Target Selection and Missing Values}
\label{sec:methods:data}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the four datasets. The Davis
dataset\cite{Davis2011} reports dissociation constants from a
competition-binding assay. The Klaeger dataset\cite{Klaeger2017} reports
apparent dissociation constants from Kinobeads chemoproteomics in cell lysate.
Kinobeads carry kinase inhibitors that capture kinases from the lysate;
mass spectrometry measures the captured proteins. With constants in nM, we
converted both datasets, as in DeepDTA\cite{DeepDTA}, to
$\mathrm{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d / 10^9)$. The
Anastassiadis dataset\cite{Anastassiadis2011} reports residual kinase activity
in a radiometric assay at $0.5~\mu$M inhibitor. We converted this to percent
inhibition ($100$ minus residual activity, clipped to $[0, 100]$). The Metz
dataset\cite{Metz2011} reports $\mathrm{p}K_i$ values from a
medicinal-chemistry campaign. Because the assays and readouts differ, we
compute each score on its dataset's own readout. We compare datasets through
the resulting rankings.

\paragraph{Target selection.} Multiple constructs of one gene count that
kinase several times. Constructs are recombinant forms
of a protein, such as mutants or single domains. The released Davis panel
lists 433 constructs. These include mutant, phosphorylation-state and
single-domain forms of the same gene (12 for EGFR), 20 lipid kinases and three
non-human kinases. For 26 of the 68 compounds, the two best-bound constructs
are forms of one gene. We keep one reference construct per human
protein-kinase gene: the wild-type construct, or its catalytic or N-terminal
kinase domain where several domains are profiled. Genes profiled only as
mutants are removed.

The 343 Klaeger targets are proteins captured by the beads and measured by
mass spectrometry. They include complex subunits, such as cyclins, that bind
the captured kinases. We keep the targets that Klaeger et al.\ annotate as
single-gene protein kinases, except Q6ZSR9. UniProt lists Q6ZSR9 as an
uncharacterized protein without a kinase domain. This leaves 217 targets and
removes lipid kinases, four protein groups, BCR and TRRAP. The source does not
annotate BCR and TRRAP as kinases and flags them as possibly co-enriched with
ABL1 and CDK8.

On Anastassiadis, nine kinases are profiled in two complexes or isoforms; we
keep the first-listed form of each. The Metz columns already represent one
human protein kinase each. We call the targets kept in each dataset its
\emph{curated panel} (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). The code repository lists each
target and the reason it was kept or removed. Supporting Information
(Section~S1) compares results on the released panels.

\paragraph{Floor-coded and missing values.} On Davis, Klaeger and Metz, several
kinds of pairs receive the same floor value $x_0$. This value is not a measured
detection limit. On Davis, the
released matrix assigns $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ ($K_d = 10~\mu$M) to pairs with
no detected binding, following DeepDTA\cite{DeepDTA}. Bosc et
al.\cite{Bosc2017} used nearly the same value ($10.1~\mu$M).

On Klaeger, we kept the 5{,}123 drug--target pairs that the original authors
classified as high confidence. We assigned $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ to three
kinds of pairs: those with no detected
binding, those whose fit was classified as low confidence, and the 194 measured
affinities weaker than $10~\mu$M. A low-confidence fit does not establish weak
binding: of the 405 such pairs on curated targets, 375 have a fitted
$\mathrm{p}K_d$ above 5. Giving all of these pairs the floor value is an
analysis convention.

On Metz, pairs that were not tested are coded at
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$, the lowest value reported in the data. The median number
of kinases measured per compound is only 21 out of 172. We therefore kept the
704 compounds measured against at least 50 kinases (median 64).

On Anastassiadis, the
$1.1\%$ of missing entries were set to $0\%$ inhibition. Most pairs on this
panel show little inhibition: $85\%$ of the entries are at or below $20\%$,
and each $5\%$ interval above $20\%$ holds less than $3\%$ of the entries. We
place $x_0$ at $20\%$, the upper end of these low values, as a chosen
cutoff for weak inhibition.

A compound is \emph{active} against a target if its readout exceeds the
activity threshold $\tau^*$. On the affinity datasets, this threshold is
$6$ on the $\mathrm{p}K_d$ or $\mathrm{p}K_i$ scale ($K_d$ or $K_i$ of $1~\mu$M).
On Anastassiadis, it is $50\%$ inhibition. With a Hill
coefficient of 1, $50\%$ inhibition at $0.5~\mu$M inhibitor means an IC$_{50}$
of $0.5~\mu$M, close to the $1~\mu$M of the other datasets. Supporting
Information (Section~S1) repeats the Davis comparison of the scores with the
threshold one log unit higher. An \emph{active compound} has at least one
target above $\tau^*$.

\subsection{Selectivity Scores and Their Implementations}
\label{sec:methods:defs}

A compound's profile is the vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_N)$ of its
readouts across the $N$ panel targets, with higher values for tighter binding
or stronger inhibition. Every score is oriented so that higher values are more
selective (Table~\ref{tab:implementations}).

\paragraph{S-score.} Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} define the S-score as the
fraction of the panel bound above a threshold:
\begin{equation}
  S(\tau) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[x_i > \tau].
\end{equation}
We negate this fraction so that higher scores indicate greater selectivity.
They reported $K_d$ cutoffs of $3~\mu$M and $100$~nM, which lie
within our sweep of $\tau$.

\paragraph{Selectivity entropy.} The selectivity entropy of Uitdehaag and
Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} is the Shannon entropy
$H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i$ (in bits) of the profile normalized to a
distribution. We negate it so that higher scores indicate greater
selectivity. In its published form, $p_i = K_{a,i} / \sum_j K_{a,j}$, with
association constants $K_{a,i} = 1/K_{d,i}$ ($1/K_{i,i}$ on Metz). It needs
affinity constants, so we do not compute it on Anastassiadis. To compare all
four definitions on the same readout, we also use a log-affinity form. This
weights each target by its readout above a baseline $\beta$; on Anastassiadis,
the readout is percent inhibition:
\begin{equation}
  p_i = \frac{\max(x_i - \beta,\, 0)}{\sum_j \max(x_j - \beta,\, 0)}.
\end{equation}
We introduce this baseline parameter; it has no published reference values.

\paragraph{Gini coefficient.} Graczyk's Gini coefficient\cite{Graczyk2010} measures how unequally
binding is spread over the panel. Its published form uses percent-inhibition
values. We compute it on the same baseline-subtracted weights:
\begin{equation}
  G(\beta) = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N} i \cdot w_{(i)}}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{(i)}} - \frac{N+1}{N},
  \qquad w_{(i)} = \max(x_i - \beta,\, 0)\ \text{sorted ascending}.
\end{equation}
On Anastassiadis, we also compute the published percent-inhibition form.

Unless stated otherwise, both the
entropy and Gini are in their log-affinity forms.

\paragraph{Ratio.} The ratio compares the primary target with the $k$-th
ranked off-target,
\begin{equation}
  R_b(k) = x_{(1)} - \max(x_{(k+1)}, b),
\end{equation}
where $x_{(j)}$ is the $j$-th largest value. The level $b$ is the lowest coded
value ($5.0$ on Davis and Klaeger, $4.0$ on Metz and $0\%$ on Anastassiadis).
In the recovery analysis, we instead set it to $x_0$. In the boundary analysis,
we vary it over the sweep. At the lowest coded value, $R_b(k)$ equals the
ranking score of Bosc et al.\cite{Bosc2017} at rank $k + 1$. We cap $k$ at 5
because half of the Klaeger compounds have fewer than five active kinases.

\paragraph{Partition index.} The partition index of Cheng et al.\cite{Cheng2010} is the fraction of a
compound's molecules that would bind a reference target in a mixture of all panel kinases,
$K_{a,\mathrm{ref}} / \sum_j K_{a,j}$. Only Klaeger names an intended target
for each compound. We therefore use the most strongly bound target as the
reference ($P_{\max}$), as Uitdehaag and Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} did. Like
the published entropy, it is not computed on Anastassiadis.

Klaeger et al.'s concentration- and target-dependent selectivity (CATDS) score
is the same kind of fraction, for a designated target at one concentration.
It requires Kinobeads dose--response curves, which the other three datasets
lack. We therefore do not compare it across datasets. Supporting Information
(Section~S6) compares a proxy.

\paragraph{Conventions.} Where the entropy and Gini are undefined, we set both
to $0$. This ranks the compound as maximally selective under the entropy and
minimally selective under the Gini. $R_b(k)$ uses $b$ as a lower bound on
the reference off-target value and scores every compound, as Bosc et al.'s
ranking score does.
Log-affinity weights with $\beta = x_0$ give floor-coded pairs zero weight.
Association constants give them a small positive weight if they are kept at the
floor value. For the published entropy and $P_{\max}$, we give them zero weight.
As a sensitivity analysis for the published entropy, we keep floor-coded
pairs at the floor value. On Klaeger, we also give every measured
high-confidence affinity its measured value, including the 194 weaker than
the floor, and the other floor-coded pairs zero weight. No data show whether
floor-coded pairs should have zero weight or the floor value.

\paragraph{Comparators.} We use three variants of existing scores. The active
count is the number of targets above $\tau^*$, or the numerator of the S-score
at $\tau^*$. A lower count indicates greater selectivity. The other two
comparators are the entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ and the S-score with
$\tau = x_0$, the share of the panel with detected binding. We compute all
three only for active compounds. Otherwise, a compound with no active target
would have a count of 0 and rank as more selective than any active compound.

\subsection{Ranking Comparisons and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:methods:stability}

For each score and parameter value, compounds are ranked from most selective
(rank 1), with ties given their average rank. Analyses of compounds with an
active kinase rank them among themselves. Analyses that compare
compounds with and without an active kinase rank all compounds. Agreement
between two definitions is the Spearman correlation between their median
rank vectors. We take the median over each definition's parameter sweep.
On the $\mathrm{p}K$ datasets, we vary $\tau$ in steps of $0.25$ (11 values)
and $\beta$ in steps of $0.25$ (7 values). On Anastassiadis, we vary $\tau$
in steps of $5\%$ (9 values) and $\beta$ in steps of $5\%$ (7 values).
For the ratio, we use $k = 1, \ldots, 5$. Bootstrap
$95\%$ intervals use 1{,}000 resamples of compounds.

A compound's \emph{rank instability} is the standard deviation of its ranks
across one definition's sweep. Its \emph{aggregated rank instability} is the standard
deviation of its ranks across all four sweeps combined. Each
definition therefore counts in proportion to its number of parameter values,
and the S-score, with the most values, carries the most weight.
We compute the Spearman correlation between within-definition instability and
each of six profile features. These are the number of active kinases, the
top$_1$--top$_2$ affinity gap, the second-ranked affinity, and the standard
deviation, range and skewness of the active affinities. A feature undefined for a compound with
too few active kinases is set to $0$. Supporting Information (Section~S3)
repeats the analysis with these compounds excluded.
These associations are exploratory. We apply a Bonferroni correction to the
24 tests per dataset in Supporting Information Table~S4: six features for each
of four definitions. We also check the Holm and Benjamini--Hochberg procedures.

We also compare changes in ranking between datasets with changes between
definitions. We matched 46 compounds profiled in both Davis and Klaeger by
PubChem compound identifier (CID). We restricted both panels to their 201
shared kinases, matched by gene symbol after updating renamed symbols. We
then computed two sets of Spearman correlations: between each definition's
median rankings on Davis and Klaeger, and between pairs of definitions'
median rankings within each dataset.

For these 46 compounds, we also tested whether cross-dataset rank displacement
was associated with instability aggregated over all four definitions or
calculated within the same definition's parameter sweep. These exploratory
analyses used each dataset's full curated target panel and original compound
ranking population. We apply a separate Bonferroni correction to the eight
within-definition tests: four definitions in each of two datasets. Other
$p$-values are uncorrected.

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}
\label{sec:methods:criteria}

A selectivity measure $f$ assigns a real number $f(\mathbf{x})$ to a profile
$\mathbf{x}$ of any length $N$. Higher values indicate greater selectivity.
Let $B = [x_0, \tau^*]$ be the interval between the floor and the activity
threshold. D1 decides which compounds are scored.
D2 concerns moving the activity boundary, and D3 concerns adding targets to
the panel. D4 concerns the direction of the score. We judge all four from the
score definitions.

\textbf{D1 (activity eligibility).} $f(\mathbf{x})$ is undefined, or flagged as
ineligible, whenever $\max_i x_i \leq \tau^*$. A compound with no target
above $\tau^*$ has no target against which to judge selectivity. D1 is a
reporting rule: a score satisfies it if its definition withholds or flags
these compounds. Bosc et
al.\cite{Bosc2017} withheld ranks for such compounds under the S-score only,
whereas D1 applies the rule to every score.

\textbf{D2 (boundary locality).} Let $\theta$ be the score's boundary
parameter: the S-score's $\tau$, the entropy's and Gini's $\beta$, the ratio's
level $b$, or the gated entropy's gate $g$. With all other parameters held
fixed, D2 requires
\[
  \{i : x_i > \theta\} = \{i : x_i > \theta'\}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) = f(\mathbf{x}; \theta').
\]
Moving the boundary may change a compound's score only if it changes which
targets lie above it. D2 concerns the score itself, not just the compound's
rank. We keep D1's eligibility threshold $\tau^*$ fixed as $\theta$ changes.

D2 allows rankings to change when the boundary crosses a target's measured
value. To measure these changes, we move $\theta$ across $B$. A pair of
compounds \emph{reverses in $B$} if
$f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) - f(\mathbf{y}; \theta)$ is positive at one value of
$\theta \in B$ and negative at another. We report the fraction of pairs that
reverse among compounds eligible under D1.

For the S-score, we check every observed value in $B$, values between
consecutive observations and the endpoints. These checks capture every score
change, so the reversal percentages are exact. For the entropy, Gini and
ratio, we use 201 equally spaced boundary values. Their reversal percentages
are estimates from this grid. For the gated entropy, we also check values at
and just below each jump, as described below.

Some scores use a fixed boundary. For example, the active count counts
targets above $\tau^*$. We assess its sensitivity by varying the counting
threshold over $B$, while keeping D1's eligibility rule fixed.

The analysis protocol should set $\theta$ in advance to suit the intended
use. For example, binding weaker than $\tau^*$ may matter at the concentration
used in practice. Different boundary values can address different questions.
On Davis, the S-score at $\tau = 5$ counts targets with $K_d < 10~\mu$M;
at $\tau = 6$, it counts those with $K_d < 1~\mu$M. Its rankings can
reasonably differ between these cutoffs.

\textbf{D3 (monotonicity under weak-target addition).} For any $\mathbf{x}$ with
$\max_i x_i > \tau^*$, if $\mathbf{x}' = (x_1, \ldots, x_N, x_{N+1})$ with
$x_0 < x_{N+1} \leq \tau^*$, then $f(\mathbf{x}') \leq f(\mathbf{x})$. Otherwise a
compound can be made to look more selective by profiling it against more
kinases it binds weakly. Tests on measured profiles (Results) provide
secondary checks.

\textbf{D4 (orientation).} Fix the panel size $N \geq 2$ and all of the
score's parameters. For every value $a$ above $\tau^*$ and above every cutoff
and baseline the score uses, let $\mathbf{v}_m$ be the profile with $m$ targets
at $a$ and the other $N - m$ at the floor, for $m = 1, \ldots, N$. D4 requires
$f(\mathbf{v}_{m+1}) \leq f(\mathbf{v}_m)$ for $m = 1, \ldots, N - 1$, and
$f(\mathbf{v}_1) > f(\mathbf{v}_N)$. These conditions set the direction of the
score. On the same panel, binding equally strongly to more targets must not
make a compound look more selective. Binding to one target must also be more
selective than binding equally strongly to all. The score need not decrease
at every step: $R_b(k)$ with $1 \leq k < N$ equals
$a - \max(x_0, b)$ for $m \leq k$ and $0$ for $m > k$.

When floor-coded pairs have zero weight, the profiles $\mathbf{v}_m$ become
both broader and more evenly weighted as $m$ increases. When they keep a
positive weight, they form a background of small weights. Turning them into
equally strong targets moves weight from this background into a few large
weights. The distribution can then become less even and the score can rise.
A score can therefore fail D4 because of how it treats floor-coded pairs.
We assess each implementation with its own treatment (Supporting Information,
Section~S4, gives an example).

D4 constrains only the profiles $\mathbf{v}_m$. On a profile with unequal
affinities, adding an active target that binds much more strongly than the
others moves most of the weight to it. A score that measures how concentrated
binding is, such as the entropy or $P_{\max}$, then rates the compound as more
selective (Supporting Information, Section~S4). Whether this rating is correct
depends on what selectivity means: breadth (how many targets are bound) or
concentration (how much binding falls on a few targets). D4 leaves this
question open.

We state no criterion for the ratio's choice of reference off-target, because
different references answer different questions.

\subsection{The Gated Entropy}
\label{sec:methods:gated}

For a profile with $\max_i x_i > \tau^*$
(undefined otherwise, as D1 requires), define
\begin{equation}
  w_i = \mathbf{1}[x_i > g]\, e^{(x_i - g)/T}, \qquad
  p_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i,
  \label{eq:candidate}
\end{equation}
with the gate $g = x_0$ and the scale $T = \tau^* - x_0$. The scale is the
readout difference over which a weight grows by a factor of $e$. It is $1$
$\mathrm{p}K$ unit on Davis and Klaeger, $2$ on Metz, and $30$ percentage
points on Anastassiadis. The score is the negative Shannon entropy of a gated, exponentially
weighted profile. The gate gives floor-coded pairs zero weight, and a kinase
just above the gate has weight close to $1$. Under the entropy's hard cutoff
$\max(x_i - \beta, 0)$, such a kinase has weight close to $0$. On
$\mathrm{p}K$ data the weights are powers of the association constants,
$w_i \propto K_{a,i}^{1/(T \ln 10)}$. The published entropy with floor-coded
pairs given zero weight has the same form with $T = 1/\ln 10 \approx 0.43$
$\mathrm{p}K$ units. Moving the gate without passing a value multiplies every
weight above it by the same factor. This factor cancels in the shares, so the
gated entropy satisfies D2. A reference value subtracted from
every readout cancels in the same way, so the score has no baseline parameter.
The score jumps when the gate passes an observed value. To count reversals,
we therefore check each observed value in $B$, a value just below each one,
and the 201 equally spaced values.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (monotonicity).} Adding a kinase with a value in
$(x_0, \tau^*]$ never raises $f$ if and only if $T \geq \tau^* - g$. With
$T = \tau^* - x_0$, this holds for every gate $g \in B$.

The proof (Supporting Information, Section~S4) has two parts. For the
\emph{if} part, every gated target has weight above $1$, and the added target
has weight $0$ (at or below the gate) or at most $e^{(\tau^* - g)/T} \leq e$.
If every weight is at least $1$, appending one weight no larger than $e$
cannot lower the entropy. For the \emph{only if} part, for any smaller $T$, a
profile with one active target and many kinases just above the gate scores
higher after a kinase is added at $\tau^*$.

Proposition 1 leaves $T$ free above $\tau^* - x_0$. Two targets whose readouts
differ by $\Delta x$ have weights in the ratio $e^{\Delta x/T}$, so a smaller
$T$ makes the score depend more on affinity differences. We take the smallest
$T$ that D3 allows, $T = \tau^* - x_0$. Results (``Scale of the Gated Entropy
and Recovery'') compares $T = 2(\tau^* - x_0)$.

Because a target at the gate has weight $0$ and any target above it has weight
above $1$, the score jumps when a target's value crosses $x_0$. For example,
on Davis or Klaeger ($x_0 = 5$, $T = 1$), take a profile with one active target
at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 7$. Adding a second target at the floor leaves the score
at $0$. Adding one just above the floor lowers it by $0.53$ bits. Measurement
noise near the floor could therefore change scores.

To measure this effect, we added normal noise to measured values above the
floor, using standard deviations of $0.1$ and $0.3$ $\mathrm{p}K$ units. We
set values pushed below the floor to the floor and left floor-coded pairs
fixed. On Anastassiadis, we added noise to every entry, using standard
deviations of $3$ and $9$ percentage points. Each setting used 50 draws.
Noise can also change which compounds are scored: a compound whose strongest
target lies just above $\tau^*$ can fall below it, and the reverse. D1 was
therefore applied to each noisy profile. We compared rankings among compounds
scored both with and without noise.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (characterization).} Let a score give each target above
the gate $g$ the weight $h(x_i - g)$ and give the other targets zero weight.
Here $h$ is positive, continuous, nondecreasing and the same for every target.
Let the score have the form $-F(\mathbf{p})$ of the weight shares
$\mathbf{p}$. Require $F$ to be continuous and to have Shannon's grouping
property\cite{Shannon1948}. This means that, when kinases are split into
groups, $F$ equals its value across the groups plus its values within them,
weighted by each group's share. If the score satisfies D2 and D4, then $h$ is
constant or $h(t) = C e^{t/T}$ for some $C > 0$ and $T > 0$. The score is a
positive multiple of the gated entropy with that $T$, or with equal weights
if $h$ is constant.

The proof (Supporting Information, Section~S4) has two steps. D4 gives the
condition on equal shares in Shannon's theorem, which then fixes $F$ up to a
positive factor. D2, applied to profiles with two targets above $\tau^*$,
then requires the ratio of their weights to stay the same when the gate moves
without passing a value. The ratio can therefore depend only on the
difference between their readouts. Only exponential and constant $h$ have
this property. For example, the entropy uses weights $\max(x_i - \beta, 0)$, with
$\beta$ as the gate. Consider two targets $1.0$ and $0.5$ units above $\beta$.
Lowering $\beta$ by $0.5$ changes their weight ratio from $2$ to $1.5$.

This result requires weights to depend only on distance above the gate.
Weights measured from a separate fixed reference satisfy D2 for any $h$.
R\'enyi entropies of order other than 1 lack the grouping property, so they
are excluded. Among scores of this form, the gated entropies satisfy D3
exactly when $T \geq \tau^* - x_0$ (Propositions 1 and 2). The equal-weight
limit $T \to \infty$ also satisfies D3.

To compare how evenly weight is spread on the same panel, we sum the $j$
largest entries of each set of shares. We call shares $\mathbf{q}$
\emph{more even} than shares $\mathbf{p}$ if this sum is no larger for
$\mathbf{q}$ than for $\mathbf{p}$, for every $j$.

\textbf{Proposition 3 (broadening).} On a fixed panel, let a profile have
positive weights $w_1, \ldots, w_r$ ($r \geq 1$) and zero weights elsewhere.
Let a score be a function $f$ of the profile's weight shares. Require
$f(\mathbf{q}) \leq f(\mathbf{p})$ whenever $\mathbf{q}$ is more even than
$\mathbf{p}$. Changing one zero weight to $a$ with $0 < a \leq \min_i w_i$
does not raise $f$.

The proof (Supporting Information, Section~S4) shows that the added weight,
being the smallest, makes the new shares more even than the old ones. The
negative Shannon and R\'enyi entropies, the largest share and the Gini
coefficient all have this property. For the entropy, gated entropy,
$P_{\max}$ and Gini, weights increase with affinity. Proposition 3 therefore
means that a floor-coded pair can gain a positive weight without raising
the score, provided it binds no more strongly than the weakest weighted
target. The condition on $a$ is needed: adding a target tied with the
strongest can lower the entropy.


\subsection{Recovery from Smaller Panels}
\label{sec:methods:recovery}

We measure how well random smaller panels recover each score's full-panel
ranking and how many
compounds remain scorable.

We drew 50 subpanels of $m$ targets uniformly at random from the curated
panel at each tested size. We tested $m = 10$, $20$, $30$, $40$, $50$, $65$,
$80$, $100$, $125$, $150$, $200$, $250$ and $300$, using only sizes below
the full panel. Every score in
Table~\ref{tab:implementations}, including the comparators, was computed on the
same subpanels, with $\tau = \tau^*$ for the S-score, $\beta = x_0$ for the
entropy and Gini, and $k = 1$ and $b = x_0$ for the ratio. We applied D1 to
every score on every subpanel.

\emph{Coverage} is the share of compounds scored on the full panel that remain
scorable on a subpanel. We average it over the 50 subpanels of each size.
Within a subpanel, it is the same for
every score because D1 makes a compound eligible for all scores or none.

For \emph{recoverability}, we first select clearly separated pairs from each
score's full-panel ranking. Their rank gap must be at least a quarter of the
number of scored compounds. Recoverability is the fraction of these pairs
that remain scorable on a subpanel and keep their order. A pair tied on the
subpanel counts as one half.

\emph{Conditional agreement} is the Spearman correlation between subpanel and
full-panel scores among compounds scored on both. We write $p^*$ for the
smallest tested panel size at which mean conditional agreement exceeds
$0.90$. This is a descriptive benchmark that we chose.

We also test whether compounds whose score depends heavily on one target are
harder to recover. For each compound, we compute its mean absolute rank
change at 50 targets, using only subpanels on which it remains scorable.
Ranks are computed among compounds scored on both panels and divided by
their number. We also measure the largest change in the full-panel score
when one target is left out. We report the Spearman correlation between
these two quantities among scored compounds.

Supporting Information (Section~S5) also reports recovery without D1, with
every compound scored. These results depend partly on the conventions for
undefined scores.
