\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:methods:data}

Our primary analysis uses two publicly available kinase inhibitor profiling
datasets, described first. Two further datasets are added below to confirm the
findings across additional assay technologies.
The Davis dataset\cite{Davis2011} is comprised of binding affinities ($K_d$) for 68
small-molecule kinase inhibitors measured against 433 recombinant human kinase
constructs in a competition-binding assay.
Affinity values are reported as dissociation constants and were converted to
$\mathrm{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d / 10^9)$ prior to analysis, following the
convention of the DeepDTA benchmark.\cite{DeepDTA}
% Pairs for which no binding was detected at the screening concentration of
% 10~$\mu$M were assigned $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$, corresponding to a $K_d$ of
% 10~$\mu$M, which represents the detection floor of the assay.
The resulting affinity matrix is complete ($68 \times 433$, 29{,}444 entries),
with all non-binding pairs assigned the detection-floor value
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ (corresponding to a $K_d$ of 10~$\mu$M).
This is the standard convention for the dataset.\cite{DeepDTA,Bosc2017} It
places non-binding pairs at the floor, above their true, unmeasured affinity.

Active entries (pKd $> 6$, i.e.\ $K_d < 1~\mu$M) make up 17.9\% of the Davis
matrix (\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}). This cutoff marks binding
detected above assay noise, a common boundary in kinase profiling. Whether a
target is occupied at physiological drug concentrations is a separate question,
which affinity values cannot answer without exposure data (e.g.\ free-drug
concentration) that none of the datasets provides. A stricter cutoff
($\mathrm{p}K_d > 7$, $K_d < 100$~nM) shrinks per-compound active sets
substantially (mean active kinases per compound falls from 77.5 to 34.4, a
2.25-fold reduction) and pushes two of the 68 Davis compounds, which have at
least one active kinase at $\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$, to zero active kinases at the
stricter cutoff (into the zero-active regime discussed in Results).
Shifting the S-score's threshold sweep up by one log unit (from $[5.5, 8.0]$ to $[6.5, 9.0]$) 
to match this cutoff lowers the S-score's correlation with entropy and Gini
from $\approx 0.95$ to $\approx 0.83$ and raises its correlation with the ratio
from $0.456$ to $0.574$ (the entropy, Gini, and ratio sweeps are themselves
unchanged by this check). The S-score therefore stays much closer to entropy and
Gini than to the ratio (\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}).

The Klaeger study\cite{Klaeger2017} profiled 243 clinical kinase inhibitors
against the human kinome using Kinobeads chemical proteomics. Its Kinobeads
table lists fitted targets for 229 of them.
Apparent dissociation constants ($K_d^\mathrm{app}$) were derived from
dose--response curves across eight inhibitor concentrations in cancer cell
lysates.
Only high-confidence measurements (as annotated by the original authors) were
retained, yielding 5{,}123 drug--target pairs for 222 drugs and 343 kinase
targets.
Affinity values were converted to $\mathrm{p}K_d$ using the same formula as
above.
Missing entries, combinations for which no binding was detected, were assigned
the background value $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$.
The resulting Klaeger matrix is $222 \times 343$.
Active entries (pKd $> 6$) make up 3.6\% of the Klaeger matrix, versus 17.9\%
for Davis above (\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}).
The 46 compounds profiled in both datasets (Discussion) are active against
$16.5\%$ of the Davis panel on average and $3.2\%$ of the Klaeger panel
(\texttt{claim\_checks.py}), so the difference comes from the assay. These data
cannot tell whether it comes from the chemoproteomic assay's detection threshold
or from which kinases the assay captures.

The high-confidence filter removes 793 of the 5{,}916 drug--target rows in the
table (13.4\%), one of which has no numeric $K_d^\mathrm{app}$. A removed pair is treated identically
to one with no detected binding and assigned the same $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$
background value. Of the 792 low-confidence pairs with a numeric
$K_d^\mathrm{app}$, 561 fall within the $222 \times 343$ high-confidence matrix.
The other 231 involve 7 compounds and 177 kinases with no high-confidence
measurement. Adding the 561 pairs to the same matrix changes only 0.74\% of its
entries, shifts every cell of Table~\ref{tab:correlations}'s Klaeger triangle
by at most $0.05$, and leaves every Klaeger panel-size $p^*$ value in
Figure~\ref{fig:panelsize} and Figure~\ref{fig:candidate} unchanged.
Retaining all 792 pairs also adds those compounds and kinases, which changes
the panel itself. It shifts the Klaeger cells of
Table~\ref{tab:correlations} by up to $0.12$ (S-score against ratio, from
$0.22$ to $0.34$) and leaves the two-family structure intact
(\texttt{klaeger\_filter\_sensitivity.py}). The filter choice is therefore not
responsible for the reported correlations or convergence thresholds.

To test whether our findings depend on the assay technology, we added two
further datasets that measure kinase inhibition by different means.
The Anastassiadis dataset\cite{Anastassiadis2011} profiles 178 inhibitors
against 300 kinases in a single-concentration ($0.5~\mu$M) radiometric activity
assay, reported as percent residual kinase activity relative to a solvent
control.
We converted these to percent inhibition ($100$ minus residual activity,
clipped to $[0,100]$, where higher values denote stronger inhibition), with the
$1.1\%$ missing entries set to zero inhibition
(\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}).
The Metz dataset\cite{Metz2011} reports $\mathrm{p}K_i$ affinities for a large
medicinal-chemistry
campaign using the same negative-log scale as $\mathrm{p}K_d$. The value
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$ is the lowest value reported anywhere in the released
data (only $0.17\%$ of measurements), so we assign it to unmeasured
combinations as the assay's own detection floor (the same convention used for
the Davis $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ floor above). Coverage per compound is highly
skewed (a median compound is measured against only 21 of the 172 kinases;
\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}). Because of this,
we restricted the dataset to the 704 compounds profiled against at least 50 of
the 172 kinases ($\geq 29\%$ coverage). The threshold excludes the most
sparsely profiled compounds while retaining a large enough compound set for
the panel-size and instability analyses. Across
the 704 retained compounds, coverage ranges from $29.1\%$ to $83.1\%$ of the
172 kinases (mean $40.9\%$, median $37.2\%$, i.e.\ 64 of 172 kinases). Thus, for
the median retained compound, most entries are floor-imputed, and fewer than a
quarter of compounds have more than half of their entries measured
(75th percentile $47.1\%$). A sizable minority sit close to the threshold: 23
of 704 ($3.3\%$) have exactly 50 measured kinases, and 170 ($24.1\%$) have 55
or fewer (\texttt{metz\_coverage.py}).

Floor imputation has a large effect on Metz. Nearly every measured value
($99.9\%$) lies above the floor, so a compound measured against more kinases
has more kinases with positive weight and looks less selective. Across the 704
compounds, the Spearman correlation between a compound's coverage and its median
rank (rank 1 = most selective) is $0.98$ for the entropy, $0.95$ for the Gini,
$0.76$ for the S-score, and $0.11$ for the ratio (\texttt{claim\_checks.py}).
Part of this is real, since compounds measured against more kinases are also
active against a larger share of the kinases actually measured, but that
association is much weaker ($r = 0.33$). No fully measured subset of useful size
exists (only 7 kinases are measured for at least $80\%$ of the compounds), so we
keep Metz as a fourth dataset but read its distribution-based results, and their
low agreement with the ratio, as largely reflecting coverage.

The four datasets span three assay technologies (competition binding,
chemoproteomics, and functional activity) and two readout types (affinity and
single-concentration inhibition).
Each selectivity definition is computed on the native readout of its
dataset, and every comparison between datasets uses rank correlations computed
within each dataset.
For the percent-inhibition and $\mathrm{p}K_i$ datasets, the S-score threshold, and
entropy/Gini baseline grids were shifted to span each dataset's active range
(entropy/Gini baselines over $[0, 30]\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis and
$\mathrm{p}K_i \in [4.0, 5.5]$ on Metz, against $[5.0, 6.5]$ on the
$\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets),
while the ratio off-target index $k \in \{1,\ldots,5\}$ was left unchanged
(it is an index of off-targets and so needs no rescaling).

We excluded two additional candidate datasets.
The Gao screen\cite{Gao2013} is a single-concentration functional inhibition
assay of the same readout type as the Anastassiadis dataset already included,
and its raw data are not openly redistributable. Including it would add a
second instance of an assay type rather than a new assay technology.
The Patricelli study\cite{Patricelli2011} profiles only a small number of
inhibitors against native kinases as a proof of concept for the KiNativ
platform. A ranking-stability analysis requires many compounds to produce a
meaningful ranking, so this dataset is not suited to the present comparison.

\subsection{Selectivity Definitions and Parameterization}
\label{sec:methods:defs}

We implemented four selectivity definitions, each with parameters whose values
are not standardized in the literature.
Throughout, a compound's binding profile is the vector of its affinities
$\mathrm{p}K_{d,i}$ across the $N$ panel kinases, with higher
$\mathrm{p}K_{d}$ denoting tighter binding.

\paragraph{S-score.}
Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} defined the selectivity score as the fraction
of the panel that a compound binds: the number of kinases bound with a
dissociation constant below a cutoff, divided by the number of distinct kinases
tested. They reported $S(3~\mu\mathrm{M})$ and $S(100~\mathrm{nM})$, for $K_d$
cutoffs of $3~\mu$M and $100$~nM.
We implemented the same fraction, with the cutoff expressed as a threshold
$\tau$ on $\mathrm{p}K_d$:
\begin{equation}
  S(\tau) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{p}K_{d,i} > \tau].
\end{equation}
Here $N$ is the number of kinases in the panel. On Metz this includes kinases
a compound was not tested against, which are imputed at the floor and so count
only in the denominator.
We used $\tau \in \{5.5, 5.75, \ldots, 8.0\}$ (11 values), spanning $K_d$
cutoffs from approximately 3~$\mu$M to 10~nM, which includes both of Karaman
et al.'s cutoffs ($\tau \approx 5.5$ and $\tau = 7$).

\paragraph{Selectivity entropy.}
Uitdehaag and Zaman\cite{Uitdehaag2011} treat the compound's binding across the
panel as a probability distribution and report its Shannon entropy. A profile
concentrated on few kinases has low entropy and is selective.
In the original definition, the weight on each kinase is its association constant
$K_{a,i} = 1/K_{d,i}$ (the share of compound that would be bound to kinase $i$
in an equimolar mixture), so $p_i = K_{a,i}/\sum_j K_{a,j}$.
We implemented the entropy on the same log-affinity scale used by the other
metrics, weighting each kinase by its affinity in excess of a baseline $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
  H(\beta) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i \log_2 p_i, \quad
  p_i = \frac{\max(\mathrm{p}K_{d,i} - \beta,\, 0)}
             {\sum_j \max(\mathrm{p}K_{d,j} - \beta,\, 0)},
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the affinity below which binding is treated as negligible.
This makes $\beta$ an explicit parameter (its under-specification motivates
property D3 below). We swept $\beta \in \{5.0, 5.25, \ldots, 6.5\}$ (7 values),
a narrower range than the S-score's $\tau$ sweep. Raising either parameter
leaves a growing share of compounds with no kinase above it (on Klaeger, 7.2\%
of compounds at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6.0$ and 55\% at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 8.0$,
\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}), where the S-score becomes 0 and the
entropy and Gini become undefined. The $\tau$ range is wider because it was
chosen to include Karaman et al.'s cutoffs. The baseline $\beta$ has no published
reference points and was kept near the $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6$ activity threshold.
We compare this weighting with the original association-constant weighting
below. % (``Faithfulness to the original definitions'').

\paragraph{Gini coefficient.}
Graczyk\cite{Graczyk2010} adapted the Gini coefficient from economics: kinases
are sorted by activity and the cumulative fraction of total binding is plotted
against the cumulative fraction of kinases (a Lorenz curve). The Gini
coefficient, which measures how unequally binding is distributed across the kinome,
is twice the area between this curve and the diagonal of equal
distribution.
The original work computes it from percent-inhibition values. As with the
entropy, we computed it on the baseline-subtracted log-affinity profile, using
the standard discrete formula:
\begin{equation}
  G(\beta) = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N} i \cdot x_{(i)}}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{(i)}} - \frac{N+1}{N},
  \qquad x_{(i)} = \max(\mathrm{p}K_{d,i} - \beta,\, 0)\  \text{sorted ascending}.
\end{equation}
We swept $\beta$ over the same range as for the entropy.
A compound whose binding is concentrated on one kinase has a highly unequal
distribution ($G$ is near 1), while a compound that binds many kinases equally has
$G$ near 0. Higher values therefore indicate greater selectivity.

\paragraph{Ratio.}
Classical pharmacological selectivity is pairwise. It quantifies how much more tightly a
compound binds its primary target than an off-target.
Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} also reported a score of this kind alongside
the S-score: the fraction of tested kinases bound within tenfold of the
affinity for the compound's intended primary target, which combines a ratio
cutoff with an S-score-style count. Anchoring on an annotated primary target
avoids the near-tie ambiguity behind the ratio's near-tie instability (Type~2,
Results) and property D2 (below). The score, however, has its own
unstandardized parameter (the tenfold cutoff) and, as a fraction of the panel,
violates D4 in the same way as the S-score.
We instead implemented the pairwise comparison from the binding profile alone,
as every measure compared here is a function of the profile (Motivated
Properties, below). We included the reference off-target as an explicit parameter
whose effect can be studied directly: the fold-selectivity, in log units,
between the primary target and the $k$-th ranked off-target:
\begin{equation}
  R(k) = \mathrm{p}K_{d,(1)} - \mathrm{p}K_{d,(k+1)}
       = \log_{10}\frac{K_{d,(k+1)}}{K_{d,(1)}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{p}K_{d,(j)}$ is the $j$-th largest affinity in the profile and
$k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ selects the reference off-target. We capped $k$ at 5
because, on the sparser Klaeger panel, a large share of compounds already have
fewer than five active kinases (median 5.5; $45\%$ have fewer than five, 
\texttt{dataset\_descriptive\_stats.py}). For
those compounds, a larger $k$ would compare the primary target against the
assay floor instead of an off-target.

This floor fallback departs from Bosc et al.'s convention, under which a
ratio-based score is left undefined when a compound has no primary target or
off-target to compare (Background). Our implementation returns a value for every
compound by substituting the assay floor for a missing off-target. $R(k)$'s D1
violation (Table~\ref{tab:desiderata}) therefore comes from this implementation
choice. An implementation that followed Bosc et al.'s convention would satisfy
D1 by construction, as the candidate measure does.

The field also assigns the concentration- and target-dependent selectivity
(CATDS) score of Klaeger et al.\cite{Klaeger2017} to this family. CATDS divides
each target's concentration-dependent binding reduction by the sum of
reductions over every target in the panel, which is a distributional
construction, and on our data it behaves like a member of the distribution-based
family (Discussion, Supporting Information Table~S2,
\texttt{bosc\_catds\_comparison.py}). We therefore represent the ratio family by
$R(k)$ alone. A larger gap to the reference off-target means greater
selectivity, so higher $R(k)$ indicates greater selectivity.
% \emph{In words:} how big is the affinity gap between the primary target and the
% next-best one? A larger gap means greater selectivity.

\paragraph{Faithfulness to the original definitions.}
The S-score and the ratio are computed in the form given by their original
sources (a panel-hit fraction and a primary-to-off-target affinity ratio,
respectively).
For the entropy and the Gini coefficient, we used the baseline-subtracted
log-affinity profile in place of the original association constants (entropy)
and percent-inhibition values (Gini), so that all four metrics operate on one
common scale with the baseline $\beta$ as an explicit parameter.
Every result reported for the entropy and the Gini coefficient elsewhere in this
paper, including Table~\ref{tab:correlations}'s two-family clustering, therefore
concerns this baseline-subtracted form.
To test whether this choice supports our conclusions, we
recomputed the entropy and Gini with association-constant weighting, then
compared the resulting rankings with the log-affinity forms. For the entropy,
this is the original form, while for the Gini,
it is an affinity-scale analogue. In Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity} the
association-constant forms are computed over every entry of the matrix,
floor-imputed ones included. The robustness check, on the other hand, restricts 
the forms to measured entries.
The two operationalizations differ substantially: for entropy, Spearman
$r = 0.42$ (Davis) and $-0.34$ (Klaeger), while for Gini, $r = 0.11$ and $-0.45$
(Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}).

As a further robustness check, we recomputed the full cross-definition
correlation matrix of Table~\ref{tab:correlations} with entropy replaced by its
association-constant form on Davis, Klaeger, and Metz. The Gini coefficient
was replaced by its native percent-inhibition form (no baseline subtraction)
on Anastassiadis, the one dataset where that native scale exists. On Klaeger and
Metz, which have missing data, this association-constant form was
computed per compound over only the pairs with a reported affinity, so that
pairs without one (no binding detected on Klaeger; not tested on Metz) are
excluded from the weighting. The Davis dataset is released as a complete
matrix, with non-binding pairs already set to the floor, so every entry is
used there.

On Davis, entropy decouples from the rest of the
distribution family (S-score $r = 0.42$, Gini $r = 0.42$), close to its
correlation with the ratio ($r = 0.34$), consistent with Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}.
On Klaeger and Metz, entropy correlates more strongly with the
ratio ($r = 0.79$ and $0.76$) than with the S-score ($r = 0.57$ and $0.29$) or
the manuscript's own log-affinity Gini ($r = 0.69$ and $0.39$). Under this
operationalization entropy does not cluster with the distribution family at
all on either Klaeger or Metz. The native percent-inhibition Gini on
Anastassiadis stays only partly consistent with the log-affinity form. It
correlates with the entropy ($r = 0.63$), but weakly, and about equally with the
S-score ($r = 0.35$) and the ratio ($r = 0.31$). So, the two-family pattern is
not clearly preserved there either.

We draw two conclusions from this. First, the two-family structure we report
throughout is a property of the operationalization we adopt, which places all
four metrics on one common, parameter-explicit scale. It does not hold for the
entropy and Gini as originally published. Second, the direction of the effect
is dataset-dependent (entropy decouples from the distribution family on Davis
and is pulled toward the ratio family on Klaeger and Metz), so no single
correction recovers one stable cross-dataset answer. The operationalization
choice is itself a source of definitional instability, in addition to the three
we characterize in Results.
Full correlation matrices for all four datasets are reported in Supporting
Information (Table~S3) and in the code repository
(\texttt{original\_operationalization\_robustness.py}).

\subsection{Rank Stability Analysis}
\label{sec:methods:stability}

For each definition and each parameter value, compounds were ranked from most
selective (rank 1) to least selective (rank $n$, where $n$ is the number of
compounds in the dataset). The S-score and the raw Shannon
entropy both run opposite to this convention (a higher fraction of panel hits,
or a higher raw entropy, indicates a \emph{more} promiscuous compound), so
both are negated before ranking throughout this paper. The Gini coefficient
and $R(k)$ already increase with selectivity and are used unnegated.
Ties were broken by average rank.
The entropy and Gini are undefined for a compound with no kinases above the
baseline $\beta$. Our implementation sets
both scores to $0$ in that case, which ranks the compound as maximally selective
under the (negated) entropy measure, tied with single-target profiles, and as minimally
selective under the Gini. A compound can be undefined at some baselines of the
sweep and defined at others. Results compares this with computing instability
only over the configurations in which a compound's score is defined. The ratio substitutes the assay floor for a missing
off-target (above): $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ on Davis and Klaeger,
$\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$ on Metz, and $0\%$ inhibition on Anastassiadis. The
S-score is defined for every compound.
This yielded 30 rank vectors per dataset for Davis, Klaeger, and Metz (11 for
the S-score, 7 for entropy, 7 for Gini, and 5 for ratio), and 28 for
Anastassiadis, whose coarser native percent-inhibition scale supports only 9
S-score threshold values rather than 11.

For each compound, rank instability was quantified as the standard deviation
of its rank across all configurations for that dataset (overall instability)
and separately
across parameter configurations within each definition family (within-family
instability).
Cross-definition-family agreement was assessed using Spearman rank correlation
between the median rank vectors of each definition family.
The median across parameter values was used because it is less sensitive to
outlying configurations than the mean.
Using mean ranks instead changes no cell of Table~\ref{tab:correlations} by more
than $0.04$ on Davis and Klaeger, and by at most $0.075$ on the other two datasets
(\texttt{claim\_checks.py}).

Associations between rank instability and binding profile features were
assessed using Spearman correlation, restricted to the Klaeger and Davis
datasets. These are the two datasets on a $\mathrm{p}K_d$ scale, so the same
activity threshold and affinity-based features apply directly, allowing the
predictor structure to be checked for replication across a second dataset.
Profile features computed for each compound included: the number of active
kinases ($\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$), the gap between the top and second-ranked
affinities, the standard deviation and range of active affinities, the
absolute affinity of the second-ranked target, and the skewness of the active
affinities.
All correlations are reported with two-tailed $p$-values. Significance
thresholds of $p < 0.05$, $p < 0.01$, and $p < 0.001$ are denoted *, **, and
*** respectively.
Analyses were performed in Python 3 using NumPy, pandas, and
SciPy.\cite{NumPy,pandas,SciPy}
A secondary analysis relating selectivity rankings to clinical adverse-event
outcomes (FAERS report rates and FDA-label discontinuation rates for the
FDA-approved Klaeger compounds) is summarized in the Limitations section.
The analysis code and full per-definition results are available in the code
repository (see Data and Software Availability).

\subsection{Motivated Properties}
\label{sec:methods:properties}

The instability patterns reported in Results motivate four properties. Each is
stated formally below, together with the failure mode it targets and how a
measure is assessed against it. In Results, we evaluate the four definitions and the candidate measure against
them (Table~\ref{tab:desiderata}).
Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_N) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ denote a compound's
binding profile across $N$ kinases, where $x_i = \mathrm{p}K_{d,i}$ and $N$ is
the panel size, which varies across compounds within a dataset
(Anastassiadis, Metz) and across datasets. A selectivity measure is a
function $f : \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}$, defined for every panel size $N$,
where higher values indicate greater selectivity. A ranking over a set of
compounds is defined by ordering their $f$-scores. We write $x_0$ for the
assay's detection floor and $\tau^*$ for the activity threshold
($\mathrm{p}K_d = 6$ on the $\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets).

\textbf{D1 (reliability threshold).} There exists $\tau^* > 0$ such that
$f(\mathbf{x})$ is undefined, or explicitly flagged as unreliable, when
$\max_i x_i \leq \tau^*$.

A compound with no binding above the activity threshold has no target on which
to ground a selectivity judgment. Its rank is then set by whatever
convention a measure uses for such profiles (Type~1, Results). D1 is a
reporting rule and is assessed as such.

\textbf{D2 (bounded gap sensitivity).} For any $\mathbf{x}$ satisfying D1, if
$f$ depends on a choice of reference off-target indexed by $k$, there must
exist $g$ with $g(0) = 0$ such that
$|f(\mathbf{x}; k) - f(\mathbf{x}; k+1)| \le g(x_{(k)} - x_{(k+1)})$.

When the $k$-th and $(k+1)$-th ranked affinities are nearly equal, moving the
reference off-target past the $(k+1)$-th kinase (from $k$ to $k+1$) must change
the score by only a small amount.
This targets the ratio's near-tie instability (Type~2, Results) and is assessed
from each measure's functional form. A measure with no reference-off-target
parameter satisfies D2 vacuously.

\textbf{D3 (distributional consistency).} Let $B = [x_0, \tau^*]$, the interval
between the assay's detection floor and the activity threshold, which contains
every defensible placement of an activity boundary. Let $\theta$ be a parameter
of $f$ that an analyst must choose on the affinity scale (the S-score's $\tau$,
the entropy's and Gini's $\beta$). A pair of compounds $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$
\emph{reverses in $B$} if $f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) - f(\mathbf{y}; \theta)$ is
positive at some $\theta \in B$ and negative at another. D3 requires that at
most a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the pairs of compounds that D1 allows to be
scored reverse in $B$. 

We use $\varepsilon = 1\%$, but 
this choice does not drive the results, since every
verdict in Table~\ref{tab:desiderata} is the same for any $\varepsilon$
between $1\%$ and $7\%$ (Results).
This targets the baseline sensitivity of compounds with few active kinases
(Results) and is assessed empirically by computing the rankings across $B$ on
each dataset. A measure with no such parameter satisfies D3 vacuously.

\textbf{D4 (monotonicity under weak off-target addition).} If
$\mathbf{x}' = (x_1, \ldots, x_N, x_{N+1})$ with $x_{N+1} \leq \tau^*$, then
$f(\mathbf{x}') \leq f(\mathbf{x})$. 

That is, adding a sub-threshold binding
interaction must not increase a compound's score. Otherwise, a compound can be
made to look more selective by profiling it against more kinases it barely
binds. D4 is
assessed from each measure's functional form.

\subsection{A Candidate Measure}
\label{sec:methods:candidate}

The candidate measure is introduced to show that the four properties can be
met together. It need not outperform existing measures on any particular
dataset. For a profile
$\mathbf{x}$ with $\max_i x_i > \tau^*$ (undefined otherwise, satisfying D1),
define
\begin{equation}
  w_i = \mathbf{1}[x_i > x_0]\; T \log\!\big(1 + e^{(x_i - \beta)/T}\big), \qquad
  p_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i,
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is a constant representing the assay detection floor
($\mathrm{p}K_d = 5.0$ on Davis and Klaeger, $\mathrm{p}K_i = 4.0$ on Metz,
and a $20\%$ inhibition noise level on Anastassiadis),
$\beta$ is a smooth emphasis baseline (set to
$x_0$ in the results below), and $T$ is a smoothing width in the native
units of the profile. We set $T = \tau^* - x_0$, the distance from the floor
to the reliability threshold: $1$ $\mathrm{p}K_d$ unit on Davis and Klaeger,
$2$ $\mathrm{p}K_i$ units on Metz, and $30$ percentage points on
Anastassiadis, whose native scale is $0$--$100\%$ inhibition rather than
log-affinity. The function $f$ is the negative Shannon entropy of the
smoothed, normalized profile, i.e.\ the negative logarithm of the
effective number of targets the compound binds.

The candidate is a gated, smooth-hinge variant of the selectivity entropy and
differs from it in three ways, one for each property the entropy fails:
(1) an explicit reliability gate, for D1; (2) a non-binder gate fixed at the
floor $x_0$, so that no free parameter sets the activity boundary, for D3; and
(3) a smooth softplus hinge above $x_0$ in place of the entropy's hard cutoff
$\max(x_i - \beta, 0)$, for D4. The hard cutoff gives a kinase just above $\beta$
an arbitrarily small weight, which is what lets a sub-threshold addition raise
the entropy's score. The softplus gives every gated kinase a weight of at least
$T \ln 2$, which rules this out:

\textbf{Proposition.} With $\beta = x_0$ and
$T \geq (\tau^* - x_0)/\ln(2^e - 1) \approx 0.58\,(\tau^* - x_0)$, adding a
kinase with affinity at most $\tau^*$ never raises the candidate's score.

\textit{Proof.} A kinase at or below $x_0$ has zero weight and leaves the score
unchanged. Otherwise, every gated kinase, the added one included, has weight
above $m = T\ln 2$. Since the softplus increases with affinity, an added kinase
with affinity at most $\tau^*$ has weight
$a \leq T\ln(1 + e^{(\tau^* - x_0)/T})$, and, by the assumption on $T$
in the proposition, this is at
most $e\,m$.

Let the original profile have $n \geq 1$ gated kinases ($n \geq 1$ because D1
scores it only if some kinase lies above $\tau^* > x_0$), total weight
$W > nm$, and entropy $H$ (in nats), and let $q = a/(W + a)$ be the share of
the total weight carried by the added kinase. Once that kinase is added, the
entropy is $(1 - q)H + h(q)$, where $h(q) = -q\ln q - (1 - q)\ln(1 - q)$ is the
binary entropy. So, the entropy does not decrease if and only if
$H \leq h(q)/q$. Because $h$ is concave with
$h(0) = 0$, $h(q)/q$ decreases in $q$. Since $q < r/(n + r)$ with
$r = a/m \leq e$, this gives
$h(q)/q > \ln\frac{n + r}{r} + \frac{n}{r}\ln\frac{n + r}{n}$. As $H \leq \ln n$,
it suffices that $\ln n$ is at most this bound, which rearranges to
$\ln r \leq (1 + \frac{n}{r})\ln(1 + \frac{r}{n})$. The right-hand side is at
least $1$ for all $n, r > 0$, because $\ln(1 + t) \geq t/(1 + t)$, and
$\ln r \leq 1$. The entropy therefore does not decrease, so the score (its
negative) does not increase. $\square$

The choice $T = \tau^* - x_0$ satisfies this condition on every dataset.
