\begin{abstract}
Kinase inhibitor selectivity is quantified using several competing definitions,
including the S-score, selectivity entropy, Gini coefficient, and ratio-based
measures, which are often used interchangeably. On four profiling datasets
spanning three assay technologies, each curated to one reference target per
human protein kinase, we show that their rankings depend on choices usually
left implicit. Moving the activity boundary between the assay floor and the
activity threshold reverses 8--13\% of compound pairs under the S-score,
entropy and Gini on three datasets, and over 20\% on the fourth. The ratio
depends strongly on which off-target it references. With entropy and Gini on a
common log-affinity scale, the S-score, entropy and Gini agree more with one
another than with the ratio among compounds with an active kinase. This
grouping fails for the entropy as originally published. We state three
properties for a selectivity measure: a reliability threshold, consistency
across placements of the activity boundary, and monotonicity when a weakly
bound kinase is added. No measure we assess satisfies the second, so the
boundary must be fixed in the analysis protocol. Monotonicity holds for simple
measures such as a count of active kinases, but not for the Gini or the entropy
with a low baseline. A gated, smooth-hinge entropy is monotone, but ranks
compounds almost identically to the share of targets with detected binding. On
small random panels, measures that follow this share recover rankings from
fewer targets than the ratio and the published entropy. Recovery is limited
mainly by the share of compounds that can still be scored.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} binding selectivity,
cancer drug discovery, cheminformatics, kinase inhibition, research methodology.
