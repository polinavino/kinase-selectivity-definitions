\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Selectivity Definitions Cluster into Two Families}
\label{sec:results:families}

Across 30 parameter configurations (11 S-score thresholds, 7 entropy
baselines, 7 Gini baselines, 5 ratio off-target indices), compound rankings
were computed for all drugs in both datasets.
Spearman correlations between the median ranking of each definition family
reveal a consistent two-cluster structure (Table~\ref{tab:correlations}).
On the Davis dataset, S-score, entropy, and Gini are mutually correlated
($r = 0.946$--$0.999$), while ratio correlates substantially less with each
($r = 0.34$--$0.46$).
On the larger Klaeger dataset, the same structure is preserved but with lower
within-cluster correlations ($r = 0.72$--$0.91$). The lower values come from
the 16 compounds with no active kinase, whose ranks are set by convention
(Type~1, below): among the 206 compounds with an active kinase, the
within-cluster correlations are $0.91$--$0.998$, close to Davis's
(\texttt{claim\_checks.py}).
The ratio definition is the consistent outlier in both datasets, with
cross-family correlations of $r = 0.22$--$0.62$. On Klaeger, the Gini's
separation from the ratio is not statistically clear when all compounds are
included (its correlations with the S-score, $0.72$, and with the ratio,
$0.62$, overlap within bootstrap uncertainty), but it is among the 206
compounds with an active kinase ($0.91$ against $0.63$, with a bootstrap
$95\%$ interval for the difference of $[0.19, 0.37]$).

This two-family structure follows from what the definitions measure. The
S-score, entropy, and Gini all quantify the concentration of activity across
the full kinase panel. The ratio quantifies the gap between the primary target
and one off-target and ignores the rest of the profile.

\subsection{The Two-Family Structure Replicates Across Assay Technologies}
\label{sec:results:replication}

The Davis and Klaeger pattern of Table~\ref{tab:correlations} extends to the two
further datasets, across three assay technologies and both readout types
(Figure~\ref{fig:crossdataset}).
In all four, the S-score, entropy, and Gini remain mutually correlated
(within-family Spearman $r = 0.72$--$0.999$) while the ratio definition is the
consistent outlier. Its correlation with the distribution-based family is
$0.49$--$0.56$ on the Anastassiadis functional-activity dataset and only
$0.14$--$0.19$ on the Metz $\mathrm{p}K_i$ dataset. On Metz this low agreement
partly reflects coverage: the distribution-based rankings there track how many
kinases each compound was measured against, while the ratio does not (Methods).
The degree of separation varies with the assay, but in every case the ratio
definition measures something the distribution-based definitions do not.

The instability findings also replicate.
Pooled across the definitions' sweeps, compounds with no active kinase
(Type~1, below) have higher rank instability than active compounds wherever
they occur (mean rank $\sigma = 36.8$ vs.\ $21.7$ on Anastassiadis, $178.5$
vs.\ $108.1$ on Metz, and $74.0$ vs.\ $31.2$ on Klaeger. Davis has none). As
on Klaeger, this contrast reflects the convention used for undefined entropy
and Gini scores, which places these compounds at opposite ends of the two
rankings (Methods, and Type~1 below). Within a single definition it is
significant for the Gini on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and for the entropy and
the ratio on Anastassiadis. On Metz the five zero-active compounds are more
stable than active ones under the S-score, entropy, and Gini
(\texttt{claim\_checks.py}).
Within the distribution-based family, the number of active kinases negatively
predicts entropy instability in every dataset ($r = -0.28$ to $-0.42$). The
top$_1$--top$_2$ affinity gap predicts ratio instability on Davis, Klaeger and
Metz ($r = -0.44$, $-0.34$, and $-0.27$, all $p < 0.001$), and on Anastassiadis
once the compounds saturated at the $100\%$ inhibition ceiling are excluded
(below).

\subsection{Rank Instability Is Structured and Associated with Profile Shape}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/instability_by_family}
  \caption{Best predictor of rank instability within each definition family
           (Klaeger dataset, active compounds, $n=206$).
           Ratio instability is best predicted by the top$_1$--top$_2$ affinity gap;
           entropy, Gini, and S-score instability are best predicted by features
           of the active affinity distribution.}
  \label{fig:instability}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:results:instability}

Overall rank instability (measured as the standard deviation of a
compound's rank across all 30 configurations) varies substantially across
compounds (Klaeger: mean $\sigma = 34.2$, range $7.0$--$92.6$;
Davis: mean $\sigma = 8.5$, range $1.8$--$19.3$).
Instability is not uniformly distributed: within each definition family,
specific binding profile features are associated with which compounds receive
unstable rankings (Table~\ref{tab:predictors}).

Within the ratio family, the gap between the top and second-ranked affinities
(top$_1$--top$_2$ gap) is the strongest predictor of instability
(Figure~\ref{fig:instability})
($r = -0.444$, $p < 0.001$ on Davis, and $r = -0.341$, $p < 0.001$ on Klaeger).
Compounds with near-identical affinities for their two best-bound kinases
receive highly variable ratio rankings because the choice of reference
off-target ($k = 1$ vs.\ $k = 2, \ldots, 5$) determines whether the compound
appears selective or promiscuous.

Within the entropy family, the number of active kinases
($\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$) negatively predicts instability
($r = -0.279$, $p < 0.05$ on Davis,
$r = -0.381$, $p < 0.001$ on Klaeger; for the Gini, see below).
Compounds with many active kinases have broad profiles whose entropy scores are
stable across baseline choices. Compounds with few active kinases are sensitive
to the baseline because small changes can move targets in or out of the active
set, altering the distribution substantially.
Consistent with this, the share of a compound's above-floor kinases that cross
some baseline in the sweep correlates with its entropy instability ($r = 0.19$,
$p = 0.007$ on Klaeger, \texttt{claim\_checks.py}).
The active affinity range is also a significant predictor for the S-score and
entropy on Klaeger ($r = -0.35$ and $-0.27$, both $p < 0.001$). Gini is not
significantly predicted by this feature ($r = -0.10$, $p = 0.14$). This is
consistent with heterogeneous profiles (whose affinities span a wide range)
yielding more reproducible rankings across parameter choices than flat profiles,
in which many near-equal affinities reshuffle as the parameter changes.

Gini instability's strongest predictor on Klaeger
($r = +0.346$, $p < 0.001$) is skewness, comparable in strength to the
S-score's dependence on active affinity range ($r = -0.354$).
Unlike the other features above, it
targets what Gini measures directly, i.e. the shape of
the active-affinity distribution's inequality.
Right-skewed active distributions are the least stable. One possible
explanation, which we have not tested, is that in such profiles most active
kinases sit just above the threshold, with one or a few much more potent
outliers, so a small shift in $\beta$ moves much of the weight in or out of the
active set. As with active affinity std and range above, the relationship does
not replicate on Davis ($r = +0.043$, $p = 0.73$).

Unlike the other families, the S-score's instability is significantly predicted
by every profile-shape feature except the top$_1$--top$_2$ gap
(Table~\ref{tab:predictors}), and most strongly by the spread of active
affinities, consistent with compounds whose active affinities cluster near the
threshold gaining or losing panel hits under small shifts in $\tau$.
This S-score pattern is specific to Klaeger: on Davis, no profile feature
significantly predicts S-score instability (all $|r| \le 0.16$,
Table~\ref{tab:predictors}, right). One untested possibility is that the two
assays place their detection windows differently relative to the cutoff
(3.6\% of the Klaeger matrix is active and 17.9\% of Davis, Methods). 
Three relations in Table~\ref{tab:predictors} are significant on both
pK$_d$ datasets: the ratio's dependence on the top$_1$--top$_2$ gap
($p < 0.001$ on both) and on the second-ranked affinity, and the entropy's
dependence on the number of active kinases. The ratio--gap pairing is the most
consistent in magnitude ($r = -0.444$ on Davis and $-0.341$ on Klaeger). The
entropy pairing is weaker on the smaller Davis panel ($r = -0.381$,
$p < 0.001$, on Klaeger and $-0.279$, $p < 0.05$, on Davis). The ratio--gap
dependence also holds on Metz. On Anastassiadis it fails
($r = -0.104$, $p = 0.23$) because of eight compounds whose two best-inhibited
kinases are both at $99.5\%$ inhibition or more: the single-concentration
readout saturates at $100\%$, so their top$_1$--top$_2$ gap is unresolved.
Excluding them, the dependence holds there too ($r = -0.275$, $p = 0.002$,
\texttt{claim\_checks.py}). For the Gini, the dependence on the number of
active kinases is significant on Davis ($r = -0.300$) and not on Klaeger
($r = -0.093$).

The skewness of a compound's active affinities is undefined when
it has fewer than three active kinases, and so are the standard deviation and
range of those affinities when the compound has fewer than two.
Table~\ref{tab:predictors} assigns these compounds the value $0$ (the vertical
clusters at zero in Figure~\ref{fig:instability}). Recomputing each correlation
over only the compounds for which the feature is defined (161 and 180 of the
206 active Klaeger compounds, respectively) strengthens the Klaeger
associations reported above, including Gini--skewness ($r = +0.482$) and the
S-score's and entropy's dependence on active affinity range ($r = -0.479$ and
$-0.396$, all $p < 0.001$). It also makes the Gini's and the ratio's weak
dependence on active-affinity spread nominally significant ($|r| = 0.18$--$0.28$,
$p < 0.05$). The one exception is the S-score's weak dependence on skewness,
which falls from $r = -0.16$ ($p = 0.02$) to $-0.15$ ($p = 0.05$). On Davis,
where only three compounds have fewer than three active
kinases, no correlation changes materially
(\texttt{feature\_placeholder\_sensitivity.py}). 

Five additional candidate features were tested:
two concentration measures from industrial-organization
economics ((1) the top-target concentration ratio (CR$_1$) and (2) the
Herfindahl--Hirschman Index (HHI)). These were sourced from the same 
field as the Gini coefficient \cite{Graczyk2010}. We also tested
(3) the raw
primary-target affinity (top$_1$ $\mathrm{p}K_d$), (4) a near-tie count
generalizing the top$_1$--top$_2$ gap, and (5) the active-affinity coefficient of
variation. CR$_1$ and HHI turned out to be near-collinear with
$n_\mathrm{active}$, which is already in the table ($r = -0.98$ to $-0.99$ and $-0.998$,
respectively, on both datasets), and thus they carry essentially 
the same information. The remaining three were weaker than the strongest feature already in the
table, in every family and on both datasets
(\texttt{instability\_predictor\_candidates.py}).

\subsection{Three Mechanistically Distinct Sources of Instability}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/binding_profiles}
  \caption{Binding profiles of the most stable, most unstable, and most
           ratio-sensitive Davis compounds. Davis has no zero-active (Type~1)
           compounds; the right panel illustrates Type~2.
           Left: Drug~64, most stable ($\sigma = 1.8$), two active kinases.
           Center: Drug~42, most unstable ($\sigma = 19.3$), 22 active kinases
           with no dominant target.
           Right: Drug~23, most ratio-sensitive, five active kinases
           including two near-tied top targets ($\Delta\mathrm{p}K_d = 0.023$).
           Dashed line: $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6$ activity threshold;
           dotted line: $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5$ assay floor.}
  \label{fig:profiles}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:results:taxonomy}

Examination of the most and least unstable compounds in the Klaeger dataset
reveals three structurally distinct sources of rank instability, each
implicating a different aspect of how the definitions are constructed.
Together they account for all 20 most unstable Klaeger compounds
(\texttt{claim\_checks.py}): 16 have no active kinase (Type~1), 2 have a
top$_1$--top$_2$ gap below $0.1$ (Type~2), and 2 have at least 10 active kinases
and a gap of at least $1$ (Type~3).
Figure~\ref{fig:profiles} shows example profiles from Davis (the
extremes are easier to see on the complete matrix there).

\textbf{Type~1: Zero-active compounds.}
The five most unstable Klaeger compounds (AZD-8055, BMS-911543, Vatalanib,
Filgotinib, ARRY-380; rank $\sigma > 76$) all have zero active kinases at the
$\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$ threshold in the Klaeger panel.
This reflects the assay result: all five are established, potent inhibitors
of specific kinase targets (mTOR, JAK2, VEGFR2, JAK1, and HER2, respectively), none of which
appear as high-confidence sub-micromolar hits in the Klaeger chemoproteomic
screen. For three of them (AZD-8055, vatalanib, ARRY-380), the target (mTOR,
KDR, ERBB2) was not quantified for any of the 229 compounds with a fitted
target in the Kinobeads table,
consistent with low expression or poor capture of those kinases in the cell
lysates. For the other two this explanation does not hold: JAK2 and JAK1 were
quantified for 7 and 12 other compounds (up to $\mathrm{p}K_d = 8.3$), yet no
binding was detected for BMS-911543 or filgotinib (\texttt{claim\_checks.py}).

Within the Klaeger dataset, all four definitions assign
these compounds numerical selectivity scores based only on sub-threshold
values, with no primary target on which to ground a selectivity judgment. The
S-score evaluates to exactly $0$ for every such compound at the nine swept
thresholds at or above $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6$, so it ties them there, giving a
mostly stable but degenerate ranking. None of the
four definitions withholds a score from these compounds, so all four fail D1
(Table~\ref{tab:desiderata}, below).

Mean rank instability for zero-active compounds is $\sigma \approx 74$,
compared with $\sigma \approx 31$ for compounds with at least one active
kinase ($p < 0.001$, Mann--Whitney $U$ test). This contrast depends on how the
undefined entropy and Gini scores of these compounds are ranked (Methods), and
within a single definition it is significant only for the Gini.
When instability is instead computed only over the configurations in which
each compound's score is defined, zero-active compounds are more stable than
active ones (D1, below). Their ranks are set by a convention. Davis has no such
compounds: every Davis compound has at least one active kinase.

\textbf{Type~2: Near-tied top targets.}
When an active compound's two best-bound kinases have near-identical
affinities, the ratio depends heavily on $k$. $R(1)$ compares the two near-tied
kinases and is close to zero, and $R(2)$, which skips past the tie, can be large
(D2, below). Drug~23 in the Davis dataset (top$_1$--top$_2$ gap $= 0.023$~pKd
units) is the extreme case, with the most unstable ratio ranking on Davis.

\textbf{Type~3: Broad profiles, often with a dominant primary target.}
Among active compounds, the ten largest entropy-ratio disagreements in the
Klaeger dataset all involve broad profiles with 14--33 active kinases
(\texttt{claim\_checks.py}). Half of them also have a large top$_1$--top$_2$
gap, for example Dovitinib ($n_\mathrm{active} = 26$, gap $= 1.935$) and
BMS-690514 ($n_\mathrm{active} = 20$, gap $= 1.985$). These compounds have one
clearly dominant target and also bind many kinases at moderate affinity. The
ratio ranks them among the most selective compounds (ranks 22--60 of 222 for the
five such compounds in the top ten), and the entropy and Gini among the least
selective (ranks 157--191). The two characterizations answer different
questions about selectivity.

Which compounds are most stable differs between datasets. On Klaeger, the most
stable compounds (XL-228, $\sigma = 7.0$; PF-03814735, $\sigma = 7.8$) are highly
promiscuous, with 55--57 active kinases: when a compound binds a large share of
the profiled kinome, all definitions rank it as non-selective regardless of
parameterization. Mean instability there is about 31--33 for compounds with 1 to
40 active kinases and falls to 16 above 40. On Davis the pattern is reversed:
compounds with one or two active kinases are the most stable (mean
$\sigma = 3.1$), and broad ones the least ($8.6$--$10.0$ above 20 active kinases,
\texttt{claim\_checks.py}). The most stable Davis compound (Drug~64,
Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, left, $\sigma = 1.8$) has only two active kinases, and
its affinity for them sits far enough above every other kinase's that no
parameter choice in our sweep moves it off the top of the ranking.

\subsection{Panel Size Dependence of Selectivity Rankings}
\label{sec:results:panelsize}

Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} scored each compound's S-score on 500 random
subpanels of each size and found that it varies more as panels shrink,
concluding that a single small panel is unlikely to rank compounds reliably by
overall selectivity. We test that conclusion directly, by measuring how well
each definition's ranking on a random subpanel agrees with its full-panel
ranking, done for all four definitions rather than the S-score alone, and on
all four datasets.

For each panel size, in steps of 30 from 50 kinases up to the full panel (433
kinases on Davis, 343 on Klaeger, 300 on Anastassiadis, and 172 on Metz), we
drew 50 random subsets of $p$ kinases from the full panel, computed each
selectivity definition on the subpanel, and measured agreement with the
full-panel ranking using Spearman correlation. Each definition uses the
dataset's detection floor $x_0$ as its baseline (entropy, Gini) or fallback
(ratio, $k = 1$), and the activity threshold as the S-score cutoff. On
Anastassiadis, $x_0$ is the $20\%$ noise level used for the candidate (Methods),
not the $0\%$ bottom of the scale used as the floor elsewhere.
We call a panel size ``recovering'' the full-panel ranking once this mean
correlation exceeds $0.90$, adopting the same numerical bar that is
conventionally sufficient to call a measurement ``excellent'' \cite{Koo2016}.
Changing the bar within $[0.80, 0.95]$ changes the $p^*$ values but never
reverses the order of two definitions on any dataset. The ratio definition
remains the slowest to recover on every dataset, and the Gini coefficient the
second-slowest on Davis, Klaeger, and Anastassiadis (on Metz it ties the
entropy at every bar). The bar decides only whether some definitions tie
(full numbers in Supporting Information, Table~S1).

The four definitions differ substantially in their panel size requirements
(Figure~\ref{fig:panelsize}). Selectivity entropy and the S-score recover the
full-panel ranking fastest on every dataset: at 50 kinases, the smallest panel
tested, on Davis and Anastassiadis, at 80 (entropy) and 110 (S-score) on
Klaeger, and at 80 (entropy) and 50 (S-score) on Metz. The ratio is the slowest everywhere, needing 410 of 433
kinases on Davis, 290 of 300 on Anastassiadis, 170 of 172 on Metz, and the
complete panel on Klaeger, where it reaches only $r = 0.888$ at 320 kinases.
The Gini coefficient falls in between: it needs 110 kinases on Davis, 200 on
Anastassiadis, and 290 on Klaeger, but matches the entropy on Metz (80). Its
slower recovery largely disappears when the comparison is restricted to
compounds with an active kinase on both the subpanel and the full panel (The
Candidate Measure, below). The Metz values carry the coverage caveat of
Methods.

This result has a direct practical implication.
Kinase profiling panels used in practice vary in size, from focused subpanels
to near-kinome panels.
On a 50-kinase subpanel, entropy- and S-score-based rankings are reasonably
stable on every dataset ($r = 0.82$--$0.97$). Ratio rankings are nearly
uncorrelated with the full-panel ranking ($r = -0.08$ to $0.18$), and Gini
rankings range from close agreement on Metz ($r = 0.87$) to none on Klaeger
($r = -0.09$). The practical consequence is that the smaller the
panel, the more the choice of definition determines the answer.
Ratio rankings in particular need nearly the full panel, plausibly because the
reference off-target (the $k$-th ranked kinase) changes whenever a subpanel
omits one of a compound's top-ranked kinases.
