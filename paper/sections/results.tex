\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Selectivity Definitions Cluster into Two Families}
\label{sec:results:families}

Across the 30 parameter configurations (Methods), compound rankings
were computed for all drugs in both datasets.
Spearman correlations between the median ranking of each definition family
reveal a two-family structure (Table~\ref{tab:correlations}).
On the Davis dataset, S-score, entropy, and Gini are mutually correlated
($r = 0.963$--$0.999$), while ratio correlates substantially less with each
($r = 0.51$--$0.56$).
On the larger Klaeger dataset, the structure holds among the 200 compounds
with an active kinase: the S-score, entropy and Gini correlate at
$0.90$--$0.997$, and the ratio with each at $0.39$--$0.64$. With all 222
compounds included, it does not hold for the Gini, which then correlates more
with the ratio ($0.66$) than with the S-score ($0.51$). The difference comes
from the 22 compounds with no active kinase (Type~1, below). We tested whether
the Gini sits closer to the S-score than to the ratio by bootstrapping the
difference between its two correlations (1{,}000 resamples of compounds with
replacement). With all compounds included, the difference is not significant
($95\%$ interval $[-0.35, 0.06]$). Among the 200 compounds with an active
kinase, the Gini sits significantly closer to the S-score ($0.90$ against
$0.64$, interval $[0.17, 0.36]$).

On the common log-affinity scale (Methods), the S-score, entropy, and Gini all
quantify the concentration of activity across the full kinase panel, and the
entropy and Gini are computed from the same baseline-subtracted profile. The
ratio quantifies the gap between the primary target and one off-target and
ignores the rest of the profile.

\subsection{The Two-Family Structure Across Assay Technologies}
\label{sec:results:replication}

The Davis and Klaeger pattern of Table~\ref{tab:correlations} extends to the two
further datasets, across three assay technologies and both readout types
(Figure~\ref{fig:crossdataset}).
Among compounds with an active kinase, the S-score, entropy, and Gini remain
mutually correlated on all four datasets (Spearman $r = 0.81$--$0.999$), while
the ratio is the outlier. With all compounds included, its correlation with the
distribution-based family is $0.48$--$0.55$ on the Anastassiadis
functional-activity dataset and only $0.14$--$0.19$ on the Metz
$\mathrm{p}K_i$ dataset. On Anastassiadis, the separation comes mainly from the
45 compounds with no active kinase (Type~1, below). Among the other 133, the
ratio's correlation with the family rises to $0.79$--$0.84$, against
$0.87$--$0.999$ within it. On Metz the low agreement largely reflects coverage
(Methods).

The instability findings largely replicate.
Pooled across the definitions' sweeps, compounds with no active kinase
have higher rank instability than active compounds wherever
they occur (mean rank $\sigma = 36.6$ vs.\ $21.8$ on Anastassiadis and $178.5$
vs.\ $108.1$ on Metz; for Klaeger see D1, below). On
each dataset the contrast comes mainly from the definitions disagreeing with
one another rather than from each definition's own parameter sweep (Type~1,
below). Within a single sweep, it is significant for
the Gini on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and for the entropy and the ratio on
Anastassiadis. It reverses for the S-score on Klaeger ($\sigma = 18.8$ vs.\
$25.6$) and Metz ($7.8$ vs.\ $71.2$), and for the entropy and Gini on Metz
($11.0$ vs.\ $24.5$ and $5.4$ vs.\ $24.2$; all $p < 0.05$).
The number of active kinases correlates negatively with entropy instability on
every dataset ($r = -0.30$ to $-0.43$; on Davis only nominally, $p = 0.01$).
The top$_1$--top$_2$ affinity gap is associated with ratio instability on
Davis, Klaeger and Metz ($r = -0.45$, $-0.33$, and $-0.27$, all $p < 0.001$),
and on Anastassiadis once the compounds saturated at the $100\%$ inhibition
ceiling are excluded (below).

\subsection{Rank Instability Is Structured and Associated with Profile Shape}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/instability_by_family}
  \caption{Within-family rank instability against the profile feature most strongly
           correlated with it, for each definition family (Klaeger, compounds
           with an active kinase, $n=200$): active-affinity range (S-score),
           number of active kinases (entropy), active-affinity skewness
           (Gini), and top$_1$--top$_2$ gap (ratio). Spearman $r$ above each
           panel.}
  \label{fig:instability}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:results:instability}

Overall rank instability varies substantially across
compounds (Klaeger: mean $\sigma = 36.6$, range $8.5$--$94.1$;
Davis: mean $\sigma = 7.5$, range $2.1$--$16.4$).
Instability is not uniformly distributed: within each definition family,
specific binding profile features are associated with which compounds receive
unstable rankings (Table~\ref{tab:predictors}). These associations are
exploratory (Methods).

Within the ratio family, the gap between the top and second-ranked affinities
(top$_1$--top$_2$ gap) is the feature most strongly associated with
instability on both datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:instability}; Type~2, below).

Within the entropy family, the number of active kinases
($\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$) correlates negatively with instability
(Table~\ref{tab:predictors}; for the Gini, see below).
Compounds with many active kinases have broad profiles whose entropy scores are
stable across baseline choices. Compounds with few active kinases are sensitive
to the baseline because small changes can move targets in or out of the active
set, altering the distribution substantially.
Consistent with this, the share of a compound's above-floor kinases that cross
some baseline in the sweep correlates with its entropy instability ($r = 0.22$,
$p = 0.002$ on Klaeger).
The active affinity range is also associated with S-score and entropy
instability on Klaeger ($r = -0.37$ and $-0.21$, $p < 0.001$ and $p = 0.003$).
For the Gini it is not ($r = -0.04$, $p = 0.57$). Range rises with the number
of active kinases (Spearman $0.76$). Holding that number fixed, range is still
associated with S-score instability (partial $r = -0.28$, $p < 0.001$) and no
longer with entropy instability ($r = 0.11$, $p = 0.11$).

Gini instability's strongest correlate on Klaeger
($r = +0.277$, $p < 0.001$) is skewness.
Unlike the other features above, it
targets what Gini measures directly, i.e. the shape of
the active-affinity distribution's inequality.
Right-skewed active distributions are the least stable. In such profiles most
active kinases sit just above the threshold, with one or a few much more potent
outliers. A shift in $\beta$ therefore moves much of the weight in or out of the
active set, which accounts for part of the association (Supporting
Information, Section~S6). The relationship does not replicate on Davis
($r = +0.176$, $p = 0.15$).

Unlike the other families, the S-score's instability correlates significantly
with every profile-shape feature except the top$_1$--top$_2$ gap
(Table~\ref{tab:predictors}), most strongly with the range of active
affinities.
This S-score pattern is specific to Klaeger: on Davis, no profile feature is
significantly associated with S-score instability (all $|r| \le 0.21$,
Table~\ref{tab:predictors}, right). On the 46 compounds profiled in both, the
association with active affinity range is weak on both datasets (Klaeger
$r = -0.31$, $p = 0.06$, for the 37 with at least two active kinases; Davis
$r = -0.16$, $p = 0.30$), so these data cannot tell whether the difference
comes from the compounds or the assays. The same compounds have a median of 4
active kinases on Klaeger and 32 on Davis.
Two relations in Table~\ref{tab:predictors} are significant on both
pK$_d$ datasets: the ratio's dependence on the top$_1$--top$_2$ gap and the
entropy's dependence on the number of active kinases. With a Bonferroni
correction for the 24 tests per dataset, only the first remains significant on
both. On Anastassiadis the ratio--gap dependence fails
($r = -0.13$, $p = 0.13$) because of eight compounds whose two best-inhibited
kinases are both at $99.5\%$ inhibition or more: the single-concentration
readout saturates at $100\%$, so their top$_1$--top$_2$ gap is unresolved.
Excluding them, the dependence holds there too ($r = -0.31$, $p < 0.001$).
For the Gini, the dependence on the number of active kinases is significant on
Davis ($r = -0.31$) and not on Klaeger ($r = -0.07$).

The skewness of a compound's active affinities is undefined when it has fewer
than three active kinases, and so are their standard deviation and range when
it has fewer than two. Table~\ref{tab:predictors} assigns these compounds the
value $0$ (the vertical clusters at zero in Figure~\ref{fig:instability}).
Recomputing each correlation over only the compounds for which the feature is
defined strengthens every Klaeger association reported above (Gini--skewness
$r = +0.384$; range against S-score and entropy instability $r = -0.468$ and
$-0.376$, all $p < 0.001$) and changes no Davis correlation materially.

Five further features, including two concentration indices from
industrial-organization economics, were either near-collinear with
$n_\mathrm{active}$ or weaker than the strongest feature in
Table~\ref{tab:predictors} (Supporting Information, Section~S6).

\subsection{Three Structurally Distinct Sources of Instability}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/binding_profiles}
  \caption{Binding profiles of three Davis compounds, targets sorted by affinity.
           Left: nintedanib, most stable ($\sigma = 2.1$), 153 active targets.
           Center: PHA-665752, most unstable ($\sigma = 16.4$), 60 active
           targets with one dominant target (Type~3). Right: fedratinib, most
           ratio-sensitive (Type~2): JAK2 and GAK tied, among 123 active
           targets. Dashed line: $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6$ activity threshold; dotted
           line: $\mathrm{p}K_d = 5$ assay floor.}
  \label{fig:profiles}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:results:taxonomy}

Examination of the most unstable compounds in the Klaeger dataset reveals
three structurally distinct sources of rank instability, each implicating a
different aspect of how the definitions are constructed. All 20 most unstable
Klaeger compounds have no active kinase (Type~1). Among the 20 most unstable
compounds with an active kinase, six have a top$_1$--top$_2$ gap below $0.1$
(Type~2) and eight have at least 10 active kinases (Type~3).
Figure~\ref{fig:profiles} shows example profiles from Davis (the
extremes are easier to see on the complete matrix there).

\textbf{Type~1: Zero-active compounds.}
The most unstable Klaeger compounds all have zero active kinases at the
$\mathrm{p}K_d > 6$ threshold. Six of them (AZD-2014, AZD-8055, AZD-8186,
BMS-911543, SGX-523, and volitinib; rank $\sigma = 94.1$) show no binding to any
protein kinase on the panel.
Their zero activity is an assay result: all six are established inhibitors of
specific targets (mTOR, mTOR, PI3K$\beta$, JAK2, MET, and MET, respectively),
none of which appear as high-confidence hits in the Klaeger chemoproteomic
screen. For three of them (AZD-2014, AZD-8055, AZD-8186), the target was not
quantified for any of the 229 compounds with a fitted target in the Kinobeads
table. This is consistent with low expression or poor capture of those
kinases in the cell lysates.
For the other three, this explanation does not hold: JAK2 and MET were
quantified for 7 and 32 other compounds (up to $\mathrm{p}K_d = 8.3$ and $9.5$),
yet no binding was detected for BMS-911543, SGX-523, or volitinib.

All four definitions nonetheless score
the 22 zero-active compounds from sub-threshold values alone, and they disagree
about them. The S-score and entropy rank them among the most selective (median
ranks 23 and 20 of 222), and the ratio, which Bosc et al.'s convention would
leave undefined for them, near the bottom (182). The Gini ranks them near the
top where its score is defined, and near the bottom where the convention for
undefined scores applies (Methods; mean ranks 32 and 212). Their pooled rank
instability is therefore high (D1, below). Davis has no such compounds.

\textbf{Type~2: Near-tied top targets.}
When an active compound's two best-bound kinases have near-identical
affinities, the ratio depends heavily on $k$. $R(1)$ compares the two near-tied
kinases and is close to zero, and $R(2)$, which skips past the tie, can be
large. On the 23 Klaeger compounds with an active kinase and a
top$_1$--top$_2$ gap below $0.05$, mean $R(1) = 0.02$ against mean
$R(2) = 0.48$. The two values answer different questions: $R(1)$ treats the
second kinase as an off-target, and $R(2)$ treats it as a co-target. On Davis,
fedratinib, with JAK2 and GAK tied, has the most unstable ratio ranking
(Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, right).

\textbf{Type~3: Broad profiles, often with one dominant target.}
Among active compounds, the ten largest entropy-ratio disagreements in the
Klaeger dataset all involve profiles with 9--27 active kinases. Seven of them
also have one clearly dominant target (top$_1$--top$_2$ gap above $1$), and the
ratio ranks these as far more selective than the entropy and Gini do
(Supporting Information, Section~S6).

Which compounds have the most stable overall ranking differs between datasets:
on Klaeger the most promiscuous, and on Davis, on average, those with few active
kinases (Supporting Information, Section~S6).

\subsection{Panel Size Dependence of Selectivity Rankings}
\label{sec:results:panelsize}

Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} scored each compound's S-score on 500 random
subpanels of each size and found that it varies more as panels shrink,
concluding that a single small panel is unlikely to rank compounds reliably by
overall selectivity. We test that conclusion by measuring how well
each definition's ranking on a random subpanel agrees with its full-panel
ranking, done for all four definitions on
all four datasets.

For each panel size, in steps of 30 from $p=50$ targets up to the full panel
(363 targets on Davis, 219 on Klaeger, 291 on Anastassiadis, and 172 on Metz), we
drew 50 random subsets of $p$ targets from the full panel, computed each
selectivity definition on the subpanel, and measured agreement with the
full-panel ranking using Spearman correlation. Each definition uses the
dataset's floor $x_0$ (Methods; $20\%$ on Anastassiadis) as its baseline
(entropy, Gini) or fallback (ratio, $k = 1$), and the activity threshold as
the S-score cutoff.
We write $p^*$ for the smallest panel size at which this mean correlation
exceeds $0.90$, a bar borrowed from the ``excellent'' range of
intraclass-correlation guidelines.\cite{Koo2016}
Changing the bar within $[0.80, 0.95]$ changes the $p^*$ values but never
reverses the order of two definitions on any dataset (Supporting Information,
Table~S1).

The four definitions differ substantially in their panel size requirements
(Figure~\ref{fig:panelsize}). Selectivity entropy and the S-score recover the
full-panel ranking fastest on every dataset: at 50 targets, the smallest panel
in this analysis, on Davis and Anastassiadis, at 80 on Klaeger, and at 80
(entropy) and 50 (S-score) on Metz. The ratio is the slowest everywhere,
needing 350 of 363 targets on Davis, 260 of 291 on Anastassiadis, 170 of 172 on
Metz, and the complete panel on Klaeger, where it reaches only $r = 0.874$ at
200 targets. The Gini coefficient falls in between: it needs 110 targets on
Davis and 200 on Anastassiadis and Klaeger, but matches the entropy on Metz
(80). Its slower recovery comes from compounds whose Gini is undefined on the
subpanel, which the convention ranks least selective (Methods). Excluding
them gives the Gini $p^*$ values of 50, 80, 50, and 80 targets.

On a 50-target subpanel, entropy- and S-score-based rankings are reasonably
stable on every dataset ($r = 0.85$--$0.97$). Ratio rankings are nearly
uncorrelated with the full-panel ranking ($r \leq 0.17$), and Gini rankings
range from close agreement on Metz ($r = 0.87$) to almost none on Klaeger
($r = 0.10$). The practical consequence is that the smaller the panel, the more
the choice of definition determines the answer.
