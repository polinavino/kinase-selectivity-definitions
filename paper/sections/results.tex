\section{Results}

\subsection{Analysis Choices Change Agreement Between Scores}
\label{sec:results:families}

Among compounds with an active kinase, the S-score, entropy and Gini agree
more with one another than with the ratio when the entropy and Gini use
log-affinity weights (Table~\ref{tab:correlations}). We rank these compounds
among themselves. On Davis, every compound has an active kinase. The three
scores correlate at $0.963$--$0.999$, while their correlations with the ratio
are $0.51$--$0.56$. Among the 200 active Klaeger compounds, the corresponding
ranges are $0.89$--$0.997$ and $0.38$--$0.64$.

\paragraph{Weighting.} The published entropy uses weights inversely
proportional to binding constants. It correlates more with the ratio than
with the S-score or Gini on Davis, Klaeger and Metz. This holds under both
treatments of floor-coded pairs (Table~\ref{tab:correlations} and Supporting
Information, Table~S2). On Anastassiadis, the published percent-inhibition Gini correlates with
the entropy at $0.63$, and with the S-score and the ratio at only $0.34$ and
$0.33$.

The published entropy's agreement with the S-score and Gini also depends on
how floor-coded pairs are treated. These pairs make up $71\%$ of the Davis
matrix. Keeping them at the floor gives a correlation of $0.34$ between the
published entropy and S-score; giving them zero weight raises it to $0.74$.
The published and log-affinity entropies correlate at $0.37$ on Davis and
$-0.31$ on Klaeger when floor-coded pairs stay at the floor. With zero weight,
these correlations rise to $0.74$ and $0.79$
(Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}). The negative Klaeger correlation comes from the
floor-coded pairs. Keeping these pairs at the floor also causes the published
entropy to fail D4 (``Criteria and the Gated Entropy'').

Using all measured Klaeger affinities at their measured values, including
the 194 weaker than the floor, barely changes the published entropy's
ranking. Among active compounds, its Spearman correlation with the ranking
that gives floor-coded pairs zero weight is $0.9996$. Its correlations and
recovery change by at most $0.02$ (Supporting Information, Section~S2).

\paragraph{Compounds ranked.} Twenty-two Klaeger compounds have no target above
the activity threshold. Including them makes the Gini correlate more with
the ratio ($0.66$) than with the S-score ($0.51$). The difference is not
significant: its bootstrap $95\%$ interval is $[-0.35, 0.06]$, compared with
$[0.16, 0.36]$ among active compounds. Each definition scores these compounds
by its own convention. The S-score and entropy rank the 22 compounds among
the most selective (median ranks 24 and 20 of 222). The ratio ranks them near
the bottom (182). The Gini ranks them near the top when its score is defined
and near the bottom when it is not (mean ranks 32 and 212). Their aggregated
rank instability is $\sigma = 77.9$, compared with $32.2$ for active compounds
($p < 0.001$, Mann--Whitney $U$ test).

Within a single definition's sweep, their rank instability is significantly
higher than that of active compounds only for the Gini ($65.0$ against
$16.3$). The difference reverses for the S-score ($19.0$ against
$25.7$, $p = 0.01$), whose score is $0$ for all of these compounds at
thresholds of 6 or above.

Sixteen of the 22 bind some kinase above the floor. Using only configurations
with defined scores, these 16 have lower mean instability than active
compounds under both the entropy and Gini. For the entropy, the means are
$7.0$ against $13.5$; for the Gini, they are $6.7$ against $13.6$. Both
comparisons give $p = 0.002$. The other six show no binding
to any curated target, although all are established
inhibitors. For three of them, the target was quantified for other compounds
(Supporting Information, Section~S3). Whether a compound is
eligible for a selectivity score therefore depends on the panel and the
threshold.

\paragraph{Agreement within each dataset.} Among compounds with an active kinase, the
S-score, entropy and Gini correlate at $0.81$--$0.999$ across the four
datasets. Each correlates less with the ratio (Supporting Information,
Figure~S1). On
Anastassiadis, the separation is weak: the ratio correlates with the other three
at $0.79$--$0.84$, against $0.87$--$0.999$ among those three. Including all
compounds lowers the ratio's correlations with the other three scores to
$0.48$--$0.55$, because 45 compounds have no active kinase.

On Metz, the ratio correlates with the other three at only
$0.15$--$0.20$, because the other three largely follow how many kinases each
compound was tested against. Untested pairs are coded at the floor, and nearly
every tested value ($99.9\%$) lies above it. A compound tested against more
kinases therefore has more kinases with positive weight and looks less
selective under the S-score, entropy and Gini. The ratio uses only the
strongest value and one of the next five, so it is barely affected. The
Spearman correlation between a compound's coverage and its median rank (rank 1
= most selective) is $0.98$ for the entropy, $0.95$ for the Gini, $0.76$ for
the S-score and $0.11$ for the ratio. Compounds measured against more kinases
are also active against a larger share of those measured, but that association
is much weaker ($r = 0.33$).

Using mean rather than median ranks changes correlations among all compounds
by at most $0.08$ (Supporting Information, Section~S2).

\paragraph{Target selection and filtering.} On the released Davis panel, the
ratio agrees less with the other definitions than on the curated panel
($0.34$--$0.46$ against $0.51$--$0.56$, Supporting Information,
Section~S1). Restoring the low-confidence Klaeger pairs shifts the
all-compound Klaeger correlations by at most $0.08$ and correlations among
the 200 compounds active under both filters by about $0.006$. Restoring the
fits makes two additional compounds eligible, increasing the active population
to 202. Every $p^*$ in the recovery analysis remains unchanged (Supporting
Information, Section~S1).

\paragraph{Changing the dataset or the score.}
We compared the 46 compounds profiled in both Davis and Klaeger on the 201
kinases shared by the panels. For the same definition across datasets,
rankings correlate at $0.34$--$0.84$ (mean $0.61$). For different definitions
within one dataset, they correlate at $-0.08$--$1.00$ (mean $0.57$).
Among the 40 compounds active in both datasets, the means are $0.72$
and $0.73$, and the Gini's cross-dataset correlation rises from $0.45$ to
$0.84$. On these compounds, the choice of definition moves rankings about as
much as the change of dataset. On average, the 46 compounds are active
against $15.0\%$ of the Davis panel and $3.9\%$ of the Klaeger panel. Without
restricting the panels to their shared kinases, the cross-dataset
correlations are lower (mean $0.53$).

\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity and Profile Associations}
\label{sec:results:instability}

Moving each definition's activity boundary across $B$ reorders a substantial
share of compound pairs. The S-score's threshold reverses $8.4$--$13.0\%$ of
pairs of scored compounds on Davis, Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and $21.8\%$ on
Metz. The entropy's and Gini's baselines reverse $7.9$--$12.4\%$ on the same three
datasets, and $22.5$--$22.8\%$ on Metz. For the ratio, $b$ is a lower bound
on the reference off-target affinity used to calculate the score. Varying
this bound reverses $3.0$--$13.7\%$ of pairs on Klaeger and $2.1$--$9.7\%$ on
Anastassiadis, with the fraction rising as $k$ increases. The ranges are
$0$--$2.6\%$ on Davis and $0.04$--$1.03\%$ on Metz.

Sweeping the gated entropy's gate, with $T$ fixed at $\tau^* - x_0$, reverses
$9.2$--$14.6\%$ of pairs on Davis, Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and $23.8\%$ on
Metz. On Klaeger, the per-compound instability under the gate sweep is close
to that under the entropy's baseline sweep (mean rank $\sigma = 6.7$ and
$7.0$, Supporting Information, Figure~S2).
Supporting Information (Table~S3) gives every value.

\paragraph{Reference off-target.} When an active compound's two best-bound
kinases have near-identical affinities, the ratio depends heavily on $k$.
$R(1)$ compares the first kinase with the second, treating the second as an
off-target (a kinase the compound is not meant to bind). For near-tied kinases
this difference is close to zero, so the compound scores as non-selective.
$R(2)$ compares the first kinase with the third, treating the second as a
co-target (a second intended target). The same compound can then score as
highly selective. Among the 22 active Klaeger compounds with a top$_1$--top$_2$
gap below $0.05$, the means are $R(1) = 0.02$ and $R(2) = 0.50$.
The appropriate reference depends on whether the second kinase is an
intended target. The profile alone does not establish this. On Davis,
fedratinib, a JAK2 inhibitor, has the most unstable ratio ranking. JAK2 and
GAK are tied among its 123 active targets (Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, left).

\paragraph{Profile associations.} Among active compounds, three associations
between profile features and rank instability are significant at
$p < 0.05$ on both $\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets before correction for multiple
tests (Supporting Information, Table~S4). The number of active kinases
correlates negatively with entropy instability on every dataset
($r = -0.30$ to $-0.42$). On Davis, this association is only nominally
significant ($p = 0.01$). The number of active kinases also correlates with
Gini instability on Davis and Klaeger ($r = -0.31$ and $-0.25$). On
Klaeger, entropy instability correlates with the share of a compound's
above-floor kinases that lie within the baseline sweep ($r = 0.22$,
$p = 0.002$). The top$_1$--top$_2$ gap
correlates with ratio instability on Davis, Klaeger and Metz ($r = -0.44$,
$-0.28$ and $-0.26$, all $p < 0.001$). It does not correlate on Anastassiadis
($r = 0.00$). Excluding eight compounds whose two best targets are at the
$100\%$ inhibition ceiling gives $-0.15$ with $p = 0.10$.
After Bonferroni correction, only the ratio--gap association remains
significant on both $\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets. The Holm and
Benjamini--Hochberg procedures give the same result.

On Klaeger, but not Davis, S-score instability also correlates with the range
of active affinities ($r = -0.32$). The partial correlation is
$r = -0.22$ with the number of active kinases held fixed. When all 222
Klaeger compounds are ranked together, the skewness of the active affinities
is the feature most strongly associated with Gini instability ($r = +0.26$).
Among active compounds, this association is not significant ($r = +0.07$,
$p = 0.31$). The association in the full set therefore comes mainly from how
compounds with no active kinase are scored. A weaker association remains
when the number of active kinases is held fixed (Supporting Information,
Section~S3).

\paragraph{Broad profiles.} Among active compounds, seven of the ten largest
entropy--ratio disagreements on Klaeger involve profiles with 14--27 active
kinases. Six of these have one clearly dominant target (top$_1$--top$_2$
gap above $1$). The ratio ranks these seven 31st to 63rd of 200, while the
entropy and Gini rank them 146th to 180th. The ratio reflects the dominant
target; the entropy and Gini reflect the number of active kinases.
Bosc et al.\cite{Bosc2017} attributed disagreements for individual compounds
to such broad profiles with one dominant target. On Davis, PHA-665752 has
the highest aggregated rank instability ($\sigma = 16.4$). It has one
dominant target among 60 active targets (Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, right).

\subsection{Criteria and the Gated Entropy}
\label{sec:desiderata}

Table~\ref{tab:desiderata} shows whether each score meets D1 to D4, judged
from its definition (Methods). None of the four published definitions
withholds scores from compounds with no active kinase, so none meets D1.
Any score can adopt this eligibility rule; the comparators and gated entropy
do so here.

The baseline-subtracted entropy and Gini violate D2, as does the ratio.
The S-score, active count and gated entropy satisfy it.
The published entropy and $P_{\max}$ have no boundary parameter, so D2 does
not apply to them. The S-score with $\tau > x_0$, the entropy with
$\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the published entropy violate D3. The other
scores, including $P_{\max}$, satisfy it. Every score in the table satisfies
D4, as do $P_{\max}$ and the published entropy with floor-coded pairs given
zero weight.

D2 does not make a ranking independent of the boundary. For the ratio, the
largest fraction of reversed pairs across datasets is $3.0\%$ at $k = 1$,
rising to $13.7\%$ at $k = 5$
(Supporting Information, Table~S3).

\emph{From the score definitions.} The S-score and active count depend on the
boundary only through which targets lie above it, so they satisfy D2. The
entropy and Gini subtract $\beta$ from every readout above it. The ratio
$R_b(k)$ uses $b$ as a lower bound on the reference off-target value. These
scores can therefore change even when the boundary passes no target value, violating
D2. Moving the gated entropy's gate without crossing a value multiplies all
weights above it by the same factor. The factor cancels in the weight
shares, so D2 holds.

The S-score violates D3 for any threshold above the floor. Adding a kinase
bound between the floor and threshold increases $N$ without adding a hit,
making the compound look more selective. With the threshold at the floor,
every addition allowed by D3 is a hit. A compound with $C$ hits among $N$
kinases then moves from $-C/N$ to $-(C+1)/(N+1)$, which is not higher.
The active count gives the same ranking as the S-score at $\tau^*$ on any
one panel, but does not divide by $N$. A kinase added at or below $\tau^*$
does not change the count, so D3 holds.

The Gini violates D3 because a weakly bound kinase can increase inequality.
The ratio satisfies D3 because a weak addition cannot displace the primary
target or lower the reference off-target. For $P_{\max}$, a weak addition
leaves the numerator unchanged and raises the denominator, so D3 also holds.
The entropy satisfies D3 at $\beta = \tau^*$, where weak binding has zero
weight. The gated entropy satisfies D1 through its eligibility rule and D3
by Proposition 1 in Methods.

D3 concerns adding a target. Strengthening binding to an existing target
can raise the gated entropy's score.
For example, with one kinase at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 7.0$ and fifty at $5.1$, the
score is $-5.502$. Raising one of the fifty to $6.0$ raises it to $-5.491$.

D4 requires that binding one more kinase as strongly as the others must not
raise the score. Consider a compound that binds $m$ kinases at the same value
$a$, with the rest of the panel at the floor. Methods denotes these profiles
by $\mathbf{v}_m$. The scores are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item active count: $-m$
  \item S-score with $\tau < a$: $-m/N$
  \item the entropies and the gated entropy, whose $m$ weights are equal:
        $-\log_2 m$
  \item $P_{\max}$: $1/m$
  \item Gini: $(N - m)/N$
  \item ratio $R_b(k)$: $a - \max(x_0, b)$ for $m \leq k$ and $0$ otherwise.
\end{itemize}
None of these values rises as $m$ grows, so all of these scores satisfy D4.
These values assume zero weight for floor-coded pairs. For the entropy and
Gini, this requires $\beta \geq x_0$. On Anastassiadis, their sweeps also
include baselines below the $20\%$ floor, where both fail D4. For example,
on its 291 kinases, with $\beta = 0\%$ and targets at $60\%$, a second target
raises the Gini from $0.0068$ to $0.0135$.

Keeping floor-coded pairs at the floor also causes the published entropy to
fail D4 at the panel sizes used here. On 363 kinases with targets at
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 7$, a second target raises its score from $-7.41$ to
$-6.76$ bits. The largest share falls as $m$ grows, so $P_{\max}$ satisfies
D4 under either floor treatment.

D1 to D3 alone do not fix the direction of a score. Reversing the active
count's sign makes a compound look more selective as it binds more active
kinases. This score depends only on which targets lie above $\tau^*$ and
does not change when a kinase is added at or below $\tau^*$. It therefore
satisfies D1 to D3. Yet it rises with the number
of equally strong targets ($+m$ on $\mathbf{v}_m$), violating D4.
The gated entropy without its non-binder gate also fails D4, since floor-coded
pairs carry weight, so adding equally strong targets can concentrate the
distribution.

For the entropy, gated entropy, $P_{\max}$ and Gini, Proposition 3 gives a
stronger result than D4. Turning a floor-coded pair into a target no stronger
than the weakest weighted target cannot raise the score. This holds for any
profile, including those with unequal binding strengths.

\emph{Counterexamples from the definitions.} The published entropy violates
D3 even when floor-coded pairs have zero weight. A profile with one target
at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 6.01$ and fifty at $5.01$ scores $-5.35$ bits. Adding one
at $6.0$ raises the score to $-5.19$.

The baseline-subtracted entropy violates D3 at $\beta < \tau^*$.
Its hard cutoff gives a kinase just above $\beta$ a weight close to zero. A
profile with many such kinases therefore has high entropy. Adding one kinase
bound more strongly, but still below $\tau^*$, concentrates the distribution.
With $\beta = 5.0$, a profile with one kinase at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 7.0$ and
fifty at $5.1$ scores $-4.89$ bits. Adding one at $6.0$ raises the score to
$-4.83$.
Adding the kinase at $6.0$ lowers the gated entropy's score from $-5.502$
to $-5.522$. The exponential weights
give that kinase only $2.5$ times the weight of a kinase at $5.1$ ($2.72$
against $1.11$). The entropy's hard cutoff gives it $10$ times the weight
($1.0$ against $0.1$).

\emph{Empirical checks.} We also tested D3 for the entropy and gated entropy
on measured profiles of active compounds. Adding a hypothetical kinase at
$\tau^*$ never raises the entropy's score on Davis, Klaeger or Metz at any
baseline in their sweeps. On Anastassiadis, it can raise the score at
baselines below the $20\%$ floor. This occurs for $33\%$ of compounds at
$\beta = 0\%$ and $16\%$ at $5\%$. It never raises the gated entropy's score
on any dataset.

We also added measured kinases bound below the threshold to random 50- or
100-kinase subpanels of the same profiles. This raises the entropy's score
in at most $0.1\%$ of cases on any dataset and never raises the gated
entropy's score.

Across the four datasets, the gated entropy's score falls as the number of
active kinases rises (Spearman $-0.76$ to $-0.95$). Without the non-binder
gate, the correlation reverses ($r = +0.67$ on Klaeger). Its rankings agree
with the entropy's ($0.969$--$0.993$) and with those of the S-score with
$\tau = x_0$ ($0.964$--$0.996$). This S-score also satisfies D1 to D4.

Moving $\tau^*$ from $5.5$ to $7$ ($40\%$ to $70\%$ on Anastassiadis), with
$T = \tau^* - x_0$ changed with it, affects both eligibility and rankings.
Among compounds scored at both values, the Spearman correlation with the
default ranking is $0.85$ to $0.999$. The lowest values
are at $\tau^* = 5.5$ on Davis and Klaeger, where $T$ falls to $0.5$
$\mathrm{p}K$ units.

Adding random noise to the measured values changed which compounds passed
D1, as described in Methods. At most 11 compounds changed status in any draw.
Among compounds scored both with and without noise, the gated entropy's
ranking correlated with its noise-free ranking at Spearman $0.972$ or higher.
For the entropy, the correlation was $0.983$ or higher.

On these datasets, the gated entropy does not improve ranking recovery over
simpler scores.

\emph{What the criteria determine.} The active count, the S-score with
$\tau = x_0$ and the gated entropy satisfy all four criteria. Within the
class defined in Proposition 2, D2 and D4 allow only gated entropies or equal
weights. The equal-weight score is $-\log_2 n$ for $n$ targets above the gate (the limit
$T \to \infty$). D3 further restricts the gated entropies to
$T \geq \tau^* - x_0$ (Proposition 1); the equal-weight score also satisfies
it. On Klaeger, rankings for $T = 1$, $1.5$
and $2$ agree at Spearman $0.985$ or higher. Supporting Information,
Section~S4, compares other variants.

\subsection{Recovery from Small Panels}
\label{sec:recovery}

On small random panels, some compounds can no longer be scored. At 50 targets,
$88\%$ of the compounds scored on the full panel are still scored on Davis,
$71\%$ on Klaeger, $77\%$ on Anastassiadis and $99\%$ on Metz
(Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}, top). Compounds with few active targets are most
often lost. On a 50-target Klaeger subpanel, a compound with one active target
remains scorable with probability $0.23$. With ten active targets, the
probability is $0.93$. Rank agreement among compounds that remain scorable
can therefore be high even when many compounds with few active targets are
lost.

Coverage limits how many pairs can retain their order. To keep $90\%$ of
clearly separated pairs, most scores need 150 targets on Davis, 200 on
Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and 40 on Metz. The ratio, published entropy and
$P_{\max}$ need as many targets or more
(Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}, middle).

Among compounds scored on both panels, scores whose rankings depend mostly
on the few strongest targets recover slowest (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate},
bottom).
The S-score with $\tau = x_0$, the entropy with $\beta = x_0$ and the Gini
rank compounds almost as the share of targets with detected binding does
(Spearman above $0.97$ with the S-score at $\tau = x_0$ on every dataset). We
call these three the \emph{share-following scores}. The entropy and Gini
curves differ from the S-score's by at most $0.012$.
Figure~\ref{fig:candidate} therefore shows only the S-score with
$\tau = x_0$ for this group.

The share-following scores recover fastest on Davis, Klaeger and
Anastassiadis, reaching $p^*$ at 20, 50 and 30 targets. The S-score at
$\tau^*$ and active count need 30, 80 and 50 targets. These two give identical
rankings on any one panel and share a curve in Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}.
The entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ needs 40, 100 and 65 targets.

On Metz, where untested pairs are set to the floor, the S-score at $\tau^*$,
active count and entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ recover fastest. They need 50
targets, compared with 65 for the share-following scores. Using benchmarks
of $0.80$, $0.85$ or $0.95$ instead of $0.90$ preserves these comparisons:
the fastest scores reach the benchmark no later than any score outside
their group.

The published entropy and $P_{\max}$, with floor-coded pairs given zero
weight, need 300 of 363 targets on Davis and 200 of 217 on Klaeger. On Metz,
the published entropy needs 150 of 172 targets; $P_{\max}$ does not reach the
benchmark at the largest subpanel tested (150). The ratio needs 200 of 217
targets on Klaeger. It does not reach the benchmark at the largest subpanel
tested on Davis (300 of 363), Anastassiadis (250 of 291) or Metz (150 of 172).
The published entropy gives weak binding a small positive weight. Including
weak binding therefore does not by itself ensure fast recovery.

The gated entropy follows the share of targets with detected binding less
closely (Spearman $0.964$--$0.996$ with the S-score at $\tau = x_0$).
Its mean Spearman curve lies below that of the S-score with $\tau = x_0$ on
every dataset, by up to $0.069$. It reaches $p^*$ at 50, 65, 30 and 80
targets on Davis, Klaeger, Anastassiadis and Metz.

The size $p^*$ is defined by mean agreement across subpanels. At $p^*$,
$62$--$90\%$ of individual subpanels exceed $0.90$ for the share-following
scores (Supporting Information, Section~S5).

Compounds whose score changes most when one target is left out tend to have
larger rank errors at 50 targets on Davis, Anastassiadis and Metz. For the
entropy with $\beta = x_0$, Gini and gated entropy, the Spearman correlations
are $r = 0.18$--$0.70$. On Klaeger, this association is weak ($r \leq 0.13$).

\subsection{Scale of the Gated Entropy and Recovery}
\label{sec:results:scale}

The gated entropy satisfies D1 to D4 for any scale $T \geq \tau^* - x_0$.
At $T = 2(\tau^* - x_0)$, it reaches $p^*$ at 20, 50, 30 and 65 targets on
Davis, Klaeger, Anastassiadis and Metz. These are the same sizes needed by the
share-following scores, compared with 50, 65, 30 and 80 at
$T = \tau^* - x_0$. A larger scale makes the target weights more nearly
equal. The rankings then approach those of the S-score with $\tau = x_0$
(Spearman $0.9968$--$0.9996$).

Recovery compares each score with its own full-panel ranking. Faster recovery
therefore does not show that a larger scale measures selectivity better.
The other analyses use $T = \tau^* - x_0$, the smallest allowed scale and
the one most sensitive to affinity differences. Supporting Information,
Section~S5, gives the same comparison for $T = 4(\tau^* - x_0)$.
