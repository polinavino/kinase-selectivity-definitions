\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Analysis Choices Change Agreement Between Scores}
\label{sec:results:families}

With the entropy and Gini in their log-affinity forms, and compounds with an
active kinase ranked among themselves, the S-score, entropy and Gini agree more
with one another than with the ratio (Table~\ref{tab:correlations}). On Davis,
where every compound has an active kinase, they correlate at
$0.963$--$0.999$, and the ratio with each at $0.51$--$0.56$. Among the 200
Klaeger compounds with an active kinase, they correlate at $0.89$--$0.997$,
and the ratio with each at $0.38$--$0.64$. On this scale the S-score, entropy
and Gini all summarize how activity is spread over the panel, while the ratio
compares the primary target with one off-target.

\paragraph{Weighting.} The published entropy, with association-constant
weights, correlates more with the ratio than with the S-score or Gini on
Davis, Klaeger and Metz, under both treatments of floor-coded pairs
(Table~\ref{tab:correlations}; Supporting Information, Table~S2). How far it is
from the S-score and Gini depends on that treatment. Floor-coded pairs make up
$71\%$ of the Davis matrix, and the published entropy's correlation with the
S-score there is $0.34$ with them at the floor and $0.74$ with them given no
weight. The published and log-affinity entropies correlate at $0.37$ (Davis)
and $-0.31$ (Klaeger) with floor-coded pairs at the floor, and at $0.74$ and
$0.79$ with them given no weight (Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}); the negative
Klaeger value comes from the floor-coded pairs. On Anastassiadis, the native
percent-inhibition Gini correlates with the entropy at $0.63$, and with the
S-score and the ratio at only $0.34$ and $0.33$. The grouping of the S-score,
entropy and Gini is therefore a property of the log-affinity form.

\paragraph{Compounds ranked.} Twenty-two Klaeger compounds have no target above
the activity threshold. With them included, the Gini correlates more with the
ratio ($0.66$) than with the S-score ($0.51$), although the difference is not
significant (bootstrap $95\%$ interval of the difference $[-0.35, 0.06]$,
against $[0.16, 0.36]$ among active compounds). Each definition scores these
compounds by its own convention, and the definitions disagree about them. The
S-score and entropy rank them among the most selective (median ranks 24 and 20
of 222) and the ratio near the bottom (182). The Gini ranks them near the top
where its score is defined and near the bottom where it is not (mean ranks 32
and 212). Pooled across the four definitions' sweeps, their rank instability
is $\sigma = 77.9$, against $32.2$ for active compounds ($p < 0.001$,
Mann--Whitney $U$ test). This contrast comes from the definitions disagreeing.
Within a single definition it is significantly higher only for the Gini
($65.0$ against $16.3$), and it reverses for the S-score ($19.0$ against
$25.7$, $p = 0.01$), whose score is $0$ for all of these compounds at
thresholds of 6 or above. Sixteen of the 22 bind some kinase above the floor.
Computed only over the configurations where its score is defined, each of
these 16 is more stable than the active compounds within the entropy and the
Gini ($7.0$ against $13.5$, and $6.7$ against $13.6$; both $p = 0.002$). The
other six show no binding to any curated target, although all are established
inhibitors; for three of them the target was quantified for other compounds
(Supporting Information, Section~S3). Whether a compound is
eligible for a selectivity score therefore depends on the panel and the
threshold. Supporting Information (Section~S3) gives the per-definition
contrasts.

\paragraph{Other datasets.} Among compounds with an active kinase, the
S-score, entropy and Gini correlate at $0.81$--$0.999$ on all four datasets,
and the ratio less with each (Supporting Information, Figure~S1). On
Anastassiadis the separation is weak: the ratio correlates with the other three
at $0.79$--$0.84$, against $0.87$--$0.999$ among them. With all compounds
included, this falls to $0.48$--$0.55$, because of the 45 compounds with no
active kinase. On Metz the ratio correlates with the other three at only
$0.15$--$0.20$, and this reflects missing measurements. Nearly every measured
value ($99.9\%$) lies above the floor, so a compound measured against more
kinases has more kinases with positive weight and looks less selective. The
Spearman correlation between a compound's coverage and its median rank (rank 1
= most selective) is $0.98$ for the entropy, $0.95$ for the Gini, $0.76$ for
the S-score and $0.11$ for the ratio. Compounds measured against more kinases
are also active against a larger share of those measured, but that association
is much weaker ($r = 0.33$). Metz therefore shows how floor imputation changes
rankings, not how the definitions relate on complete profiles.

\paragraph{Target selection and dataset.} On the released Davis panel, the
ratio agrees less with the other definitions than on the curated panel
($0.34$--$0.46$ against $0.51$--$0.56$; Supporting Information,
Section~S1). On the 46 compounds profiled in both Davis and Klaeger, the same
definition's Davis and Klaeger rankings correlate
at $0.33$--$0.84$ (mean $0.60$), and different definitions' rankings within one
dataset at $-0.09$--$1.00$ (mean $0.56$). Among the 40 compounds active in both
datasets, the means are $0.72$ and $0.73$, and the Gini's cross-dataset
correlation rises from $0.45$ to $0.84$. On these compounds the choice of
definition moves rankings about as much as the change of dataset. The same
compounds are active against $15.0\%$ of the Davis panel and $3.9\%$ of the
Klaeger panel on average, and without restricting the panels to their shared
kinases the cross-dataset correlations are lower (mean $0.53$). Restoring the
low-confidence Klaeger pairs shifts the all-compound Klaeger correlations by at
most $0.08$ and the active-compound correlations by at most $0.01$, and leaves
every $p^*$ of the recovery analysis unchanged (Supporting Information,
Section~S1).

\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity and Profile Associations}
\label{sec:results:instability}

Moving each definition's activity boundary across $B$ reorders a substantial
share of compound pairs. The S-score's threshold reverses $8.4$--$13.0\%$ of
pairs of scored compounds on Davis, Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and $21.8\%$ on
Metz. The entropy's and Gini's baselines reverse $7.9$--$12.4\%$, and
$22.5$--$22.8\%$ on Metz. The ratio's level $b$, which replaces any
off-target value below $b$, measured or not, reverses $2.1$--$13.7\%$ on
Klaeger and Anastassiadis depending on $k$, $0$--$2.6\%$ on Davis and
$0.04$--$1.03\%$ on Metz. Sweeping the gated entropy's gate $x_0$, with
$\beta = x_0$ and $T$ fixed at $\tau^* - x_0$, reverses $9.2$--$14.2\%$ of
pairs, and $23.6\%$ on Metz. On Klaeger its per-compound instability
matches that of the entropy's baseline (mean rank $\sigma = 6.8$ and $7.0$;
Supporting Information, Figure~S2). With the gate fixed, its remaining
parameter, the emphasis baseline, reverses at most $0.72\%$ of pairs.
Supporting Information (Table~S3) gives every value.

\paragraph{Reference off-target.} When an active compound's two best-bound
kinases have near-identical affinities, the ratio depends heavily on $k$.
$R(1)$ compares the two near-tied kinases and is close to zero, and $R(2)$,
which skips past the tie, can be large. On the 22 Klaeger compounds with an
active kinase and a top$_1$--top$_2$ gap below $0.05$, mean $R(1) = 0.02$
against $R(2) = 0.50$. The two values answer different questions: $R(1)$
treats the second kinase as an off-target, and $R(2)$ treats it as a co-target.
On Davis, fedratinib, with JAK2 and GAK tied, has the most unstable ratio
ranking (Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, left).

\paragraph{Profile associations.} Ranked among compounds with an active kinase,
three associations between profile features and instability hold on both
$\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets (Supporting Information, Table~S4). The number of
active kinases correlates negatively with entropy instability on every dataset
($r = -0.30$ to $-0.42$; on Davis only nominally, $p = 0.01$) and with Gini
instability on Davis and Klaeger ($r = -0.31$ and $-0.25$). A possible reason
is that moving the baseline changes the active set of such compounds. On
Klaeger, entropy instability correlates with the share of a compound's
above-floor kinases that lie within the baseline sweep ($r = 0.22$,
$p = 0.002$). The top$_1$--top$_2$ gap
correlates with ratio instability on Davis, Klaeger and Metz ($r = -0.44$,
$-0.28$ and $-0.26$, all $p < 0.001$), but not on Anastassiadis ($r = 0.00$;
$-0.15$, $p = 0.10$, without eight compounds whose two best targets are at the
$100\%$ inhibition ceiling). With a Bonferroni correction, only the ratio--gap
association remains significant on both $\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets. On Klaeger,
S-score instability also correlates with the range of active affinities
($r = -0.32$; partial $r = -0.22$ with the number of active kinases held
fixed), but not on Davis. Ranked among all 222 Klaeger compounds, the skewness
of the active affinities was the strongest correlate of Gini instability
($r = +0.26$); ranked among active compounds it is not significant ($r = +0.07$,
$p = 0.31$), so the marginal association came mainly from the scoring of
compounds with no active kinase. With the number of active kinases held fixed,
a weaker association remains (Supporting Information, Section~S3).

\paragraph{Broad profiles.} Among active compounds, seven of the ten largest
entropy--ratio disagreements on Klaeger involve profiles with 14--27 active
kinases, and six of these have one clearly dominant target (top$_1$--top$_2$
gap above $1$). The ratio ranks these seven 31st to 63rd of 200 and the
entropy and Gini 146th to 180th: the ratio reflects the dominant target, and the
entropy and Gini the number of active kinases. Bosc et al.\cite{Bosc2017}
described such profiles for individual compounds. On Davis, the most unstable
compound overall is a profile of this kind (Figure~\ref{fig:profiles},
right).

\subsection{Monotonicity Under Weak-Target Addition}
\label{sec:desiderata}

Table~\ref{tab:desiderata} summarizes the criteria. None of the four
definitions has an eligibility rule, and any score can adopt one. No boundary
parameter meets D2 on every dataset, and the verdicts are the same for any
$\varepsilon$ up to $13\%$ (Supporting Information, Table~S3). The
published entropy and $P_{\max}$ have no boundary parameter and are not
assessed against D2. D3 separates the scores.

The S-score violates D3 for any threshold above the floor: adding a kinase
bound between the floor and the threshold increases $N$ but not the number of
hits, so the compound looks more selective. With the threshold at the floor,
every addition that D3 allows is a hit, and a compound with $C$ hits among $N$
kinases moves from $-C/N$ to $-(C+1)/(N+1)$, which is not higher. The active
count ranks every panel as the S-score at $\tau^*$ does but does not divide by
$N$, so it satisfies D3. D3 therefore concerns how scores change when a panel
is extended, not how compounds are ranked on one panel. The Gini violates D3,
since a weakly bound kinase can raise its inequality. The ratio satisfies it,
because a weak addition cannot displace the primary target or lower the
reference off-target. $P_{\max}$ satisfies it too: a weak addition leaves its
numerator unchanged and raises its denominator. The published entropy violates
it: with floor-coded pairs given no weight, a profile with one target at
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 6.01$ and fifty at $5.01$ scores $-5.35$ bits, and adding one
at $6.0$ raises this to $-5.19$.

The entropy satisfies D3 at $\beta = \tau^*$, where weak binding carries no
weight, but not at $\beta < \tau^*$. Its hard cutoff gives a kinase just above
$\beta$ a weight close to zero, so a profile with many such kinases has high
entropy, and adding one kinase bound more strongly, but still below $\tau^*$,
concentrates the distribution. With $\beta = 5.0$, a profile with one kinase at
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 7.0$ and fifty at $5.1$ scores $-4.89$ bits, and adding one at
$6.0$ raises this to $-4.83$. Adding one kinase at $\tau^*$ never raises the
entropy's score on Davis, Klaeger or Metz at any baseline in their sweeps. On
Anastassiadis it does at baselines below the $20\%$ floor ($33\%$ of the
compounds at $\beta = 0\%$, $16\%$ at $5\%$). Adding real kinases bound below
the threshold to random 50- or 100-kinase panels raises the score in at most
$0.1\%$ of cases on any dataset.

The gated entropy satisfies D1 by construction and D3 by the proposition in
Methods, while weighting binding between the floor and the threshold
continuously. D3 also holds for every scored compound on all four datasets
when one kinase is added at $\tau^*$. Its score falls with the number of active
kinases (Spearman $-0.78$ to $-0.97$); without the non-binder gate the
floor-coded pairs dominate and the score rises with it ($r = +0.91$ on
Klaeger). It ranks the compounds almost exactly as the entropy does
($0.994$--$0.998$) and as the S-score with $\tau = x_0$ does
($0.998$--$0.9997$), which also satisfies D1 and D3. Moving $\tau^*$ from $5.5$ to $7$ ($40\%$ to $70\%$ on Anastassiadis)
changes which compounds are scored but not how those scored at both values are
ranked (Spearman $\geq 0.999$). Under the noise model of Methods, D1 changed
the status of at most 11 compounds in any draw, and the ranking of the
compounds scored with and without noise stayed at Spearman $0.978$ or higher
with the noise-free ranking, against $0.983$ or higher for the entropy. This tests noise in
measured values, not uncertainty about which floor-coded pairs bind above the
floor. The guarantee concerns adding a target: strengthening a target already
in the profile can raise the score (one target at $7$ and fifty at $5.1$,
with one of the fifty raised to $6.0$, moves from $-5.642$ to $-5.637$). What
the gated entropy adds is a Shannon entropy with a proof of D3 and continuous
weights; on these data it is not an empirical improvement on simpler scores.
Variants with other R\'enyi orders, smoothing widths and floors are in
Supporting Information (Section~S4).

\subsection{Recovery from Small Panels}
\label{sec:recovery}

On small random panels, some compounds can no longer be scored. At 50 targets,
$88\%$ of the compounds scored on the full panel are still scored on Davis,
$71\%$ on Klaeger, $77\%$ on Anastassiadis and $99\%$ on Metz
(Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}, top). Coverage limits the recovery of pair
order. Keeping $90\%$ of clearly separated pairs takes 150 targets on Davis,
200 on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and 40 on Metz for every score except the
ratio, the published entropy and $P_{\max}$, which need as many or more
(Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}, middle).

Among compounds scored on both panels, scores whose ranking depends mostly on
the few strongest targets recover slowest (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate},
bottom).
The S-score with $\tau = x_0$, the entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the
gated entropy rank compounds almost by the share of targets with detected
binding (Spearman above $0.97$ with the S-score at $\tau = x_0$ on every
dataset). They reach $p^*$ at 20 targets on Davis, 50 on Klaeger and 30 on
Anastassiadis. The S-score at $\tau^*$ and the active count need 30, 80 and 50,
and the entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ 40, 100 and 65. On Metz, where untested
targets are set to the floor, the S-score at $\tau^*$ and the active count
reach $p^*$ at 50 targets, before the share-following scores (65). The
published entropy and $P_{\max}$, with floor-coded pairs given no weight, need
300 of 363 targets on Davis and 200 of 217 on Klaeger; on Metz the published
entropy needs 150 of 172 and $P_{\max}$ more than the largest subpanel tested
(150). The ratio needs 200 of 217 targets on Klaeger and more than the
largest subpanel tested on the other datasets (300 of 363, 250 of 291 and 150
of 172). The published entropy gives weak binding a small positive weight, so
including weak binding does not by itself make a score recover quickly. The
gated entropy recovers rankings no better than the S-score with $\tau = x_0$.

$p^*$ is a threshold on the mean: at $p^*$, $62$--$90\%$ of individual
subpanels exceed $0.90$ for the share-following scores (Supporting
Information, Section~S5). The active count and the S-score at $\tau^*$ give
identical recovery on every dataset, although only the count satisfies D3. On
Davis, Anastassiadis and Metz, compounds whose score changes most when one
target is left out tend to have larger rank errors at 50 targets (Spearman
$r = 0.21$--$0.73$ for the entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the gated
entropy); on Klaeger this association is weak ($r \leq 0.14$).
