\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Kinase inhibitors are among the most intensely investigated compound classes
in drug discovery, with 94 FDA-approved agents and several hundred in
clinical development.\cite{Roskoski2026}
Because the human kinome is comprised of more than 500 proteins sharing a
highly conserved ATP-binding site,\cite{Manning2002} the design of inhibitors
with controlled target profiles remains a central challenge.
Selectivity is a primary
criterion in lead optimization and a key determinant in the drug approval
process.\cite{Bhullar2018}

Despite its importance, no single consistent definition of selectivity exists.
The field employs at least four distinct quantitative measures:
the S-score,\cite{Karaman2008} selectivity entropy,\cite{Uitdehaag2011}
Gini coefficient,\cite{Graczyk2010} and ratio-based measures, which compare the
primary target with an off-target.
These definitions are often used interchangeably, and their parameter values
(the threshold in the S-score, the baseline in the entropy and Gini
coefficient, the reference off-target in ratio-based measures) are usually set
by convention.
Standardizing them would remove one source of disagreement between
laboratories and platforms.

Bosc et al.\cite{Bosc2017} showed that these definitions can produce
substantially different compound rankings on the same dataset, and the
KInhibition platform\cite{KInhibition2018} explicitly acknowledged that
conflicting results across metrics are common.
To our knowledge, which binding profile properties determine when the
definitions agree or conflict, and why, has not been characterized.
Go/no-go decisions in lead optimization are often made on selectivity
assessments whose sensitivity to the choice of definition is unknown, and
inconsistent definitions make compounds hard to compare across studies.

In information theory, Shannon\cite{Shannon1948} settled which function should
measure entropy by writing down a few properties a sensible measure must
have and showing that only one formula satisfies them. We follow this
precedent in part: we characterize how the competing measures behave, state
properties a good measure should have, and examine which formulas satisfy
them. We use four profiling datasets that span three assay technologies, each
curated to one reference target per human protein kinase. Our contributions are
as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[(i)] we show that, with entropy and Gini on a common log-affinity
scale, the S-score, entropy and Gini (the distribution-based family) agree more
with each other than with the fold-selectivity ratio $R(k)$, clearly on the two
$\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets among compounds with an active kinase and with
qualifications on the other two. With the entropy in its original
association-constant form, this grouping fails on all three affinity datasets
(Methods);

    \item[(ii)] we identify three sources of rank instability: near-tied top
targets, broad profiles, often with one dominant target, and, for compounds with
no binding above the activity threshold, the convention each definition uses to
score them;

    \item[(iii)] we state three properties, (D1) a reliability threshold, (D2)
distributional consistency and (D3) monotonicity under weak off-target addition.
No measure examined satisfies D2: moving the activity boundary between the
assay floor and the activity threshold reverses 8--13\% of compound pairs under
the S-score, entropy and Gini on three datasets. D3 holds for simple measures,
such as a count of active kinases, but not for the S-score, the Gini, or the
entropy with a baseline below the activity threshold;

    \item[(iv)] we give a gated, smooth-hinge entropy that provably satisfies D3
while still weighting binding below the activity threshold. It ranks these
datasets almost identically to the entropy;

    \item[(v)] we evaluate recovery from random subpanels separately from the
properties. Measures that weight weak binding recover rankings from fewer
targets on three of the four datasets, but on small panels the share of
compounds that can be scored limits recovery more than the choice of measure.
\end{itemize}
