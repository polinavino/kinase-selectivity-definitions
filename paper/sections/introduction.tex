\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Kinase inhibitors are among the most investigated compound classes
in drug discovery, with 94 agents approved by the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration (FDA) and several hundred in
clinical development\cite{Roskoski2026}. The human kinome is comprised of more than
500 proteins with a conserved adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-binding site\cite{Manning2002}, and
selectivity is a primary criterion in lead optimization\cite{Bhullar2018}.
Profiling assays measure a compound against a panel of tens to hundreds of
kinases, and the resulting profile is often reduced to one selectivity score.
Several scores are in use:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the S-score\cite{Karaman2008}, the fraction of the panel bound below an
        affinity cutoff
  \item the selectivity entropy\cite{Uitdehaag2011}, the Shannon entropy of the
        profile treated as a distribution
  \item the Gini coefficient\cite{Graczyk2010} of the profile
  \item the partition index\cite{Cheng2010}, the share of binding that falls on
        one target
  \item ratio-based measures, which compare the primary target with an
        off-target.
\end{itemize}
They are often used interchangeably, although each summarizes a different
aspect of the profile.

Earlier work established that these scores can disagree. Bosc et
al.\cite{Bosc2017} compared six scores on the Davis and Anastassiadis datasets,
computed several at more than one parameter value, then compared them on compounds
and kinases shared between datasets. They found that compound rankings differ
substantially between them. They attributed individual
disagreements to profile shape, such as a broad binder with one dominant
target. The KInhibition platform\cite{KInhibition2018} noted that conflicting
results across metrics are common, and introduced a composite score. A
later resource\cite{Gujral2026} from the same group offers a choice of scores.
Miljkovic and Bajorath\cite{Bajorath2018} found the concentration-dependent
selectivity score of Klaeger et al.\cite{Klaeger2017} broadly consistent with
activity data from ChEMBL. Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} and Uitdehaag et
al.\cite{Uitdehaag2012} showed that the S-score and the entropy of individual
compounds vary when they are computed on random subpanels.

These studies establish disagreement between scores and sensitivity to
parameters and panel composition. We examine how score agreement changes
when affinity weighting, floor-coded pairs, compound eligibility and target
selection are varied explicitly. On smaller panels, we assess both how well
compounds that remain scorable retain their full-panel ranking and what
fraction of the full panel's scorable compounds can still be scored.
Rank agreement can remain high even when compounds with few active targets
become unscorable.

Our primary contribution is an empirical comparison of these analysis choices,
showing how ranking recovery on smaller panels depends on which compounds
remain scorable. We compare
the S-score, entropy, Gini and ratio on four datasets that span three assay technologies.
Each is curated to one reference target per human protein kinase, and the
entropy and Gini are compared in baseline-subtracted and published forms
where the required readout is available. We vary the compounds ranked, the treatment of
floor-coded pairs, and each score's parameter, then relate the
resulting instability to features of the binding profile as exploratory
diagnostics. Motivated by these sensitivities, we propose and defend four design and
reporting criteria for a selectivity measure: activity eligibility,
locality of the activity boundary, monotonicity when a weakly bound kinase is
added, and orientation on profiles with equally strong targets. We evaluate
the existing scores and simple comparators against them.

Our second contribution
is a conditional characterization of gated entropies that satisfy all four.
We consider scores that weight each target according to its distance above
the activity boundary. We require the same result whether targets are
evaluated all at once or in groups: the score across groups plus the scores
within each group, weighted by each group's share of the total weight.
Under these assumptions, the criteria allow only exponential weights (including the limiting case
in which every target above the boundary receives the same weight). At finite
scales, the exponential family has a single parameter, the
\emph{scale}, the readout difference over which a weight grows by a factor
of $e$. Monotonicity fixes the range of the scale. Within that range, the
scale can be chosen so that the score recovers rankings from small panels as
fast as the share of panel entries above the operational floor, but
an empirical recovery advantage over existing measures is not identified.
