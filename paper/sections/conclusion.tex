\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have characterized four kinase inhibitor selectivity definitions on four
profiling datasets, each curated to one reference target per human protein
kinase. Their rankings depend on choices usually left implicit: where the
activity boundary is placed, which off-target the ratio references, how
compounds with no binding above the activity threshold are scored, how the
entropy and Gini are operationalized, and which assay targets count as
kinases. Rank instability is associated with binding profile shape, and among
the most unstable compounds it has three structurally distinct sources. On a
common log-affinity scale, the S-score, entropy and Gini agree more with one
another than with the ratio, most clearly on the two $\mathrm{p}K_d$ datasets.
Under the association-constant form of the original publication, the entropy
does not cluster with the S-score and Gini, and how far it departs from them
depends on how pairs without detected binding are treated.

We stated three properties for a selectivity measure: a reliability threshold,
distributional consistency across placements of the activity boundary, and
monotonicity under weak off-target addition. No measure we assess satisfies
distributional consistency. Moving the activity boundary between the assay
floor and the activity threshold reverses 8--13\% of compound pairs under the
S-score, entropy and Gini on three datasets, and over 20\% on the fourth. The
boundary must therefore be fixed in the analysis protocol and its effect
reported. The reliability threshold is a reporting rule that any measure can
adopt. Monotonicity separates the measures: it holds for the ratio and for
simple measures such as a count of active kinases, but not for the S-score with
a threshold above the floor, the Gini, or the entropy with a baseline below the
activity threshold.

The candidate we propose, a gated, smooth-hinge entropy, satisfies the
reliability threshold and monotonicity, which we prove, and weights binding
below the activity threshold continuously. On these data it ranks compounds
almost identically to the entropy and to the share of targets with detected
binding, which is also monotone, so it is not an empirical improvement on
either. Recovery from small panels is a separate objective. Measures that
follow the share of targets with detected binding, the candidate among them,
recover rankings from fewer targets than the ratio and the published entropy,
which rest on the strongest targets. On small random panels, recovery is
limited mainly by the share of compounds that can be scored.

The immediate practical implications are that selectivity assessments should
state their unit of analysis, fix the activity boundary in advance and report
how rankings change with it, withhold scores from compounds with no binding
above the activity threshold, and report how many compounds remain scorable on
small panels. The longer-term implication is that the field needs a principled
basis for choosing among selectivity definitions. Establishing properties that
single out a measure, as Shannon's axioms do for entropy, is a natural next
step, for which additional datasets may be required.
