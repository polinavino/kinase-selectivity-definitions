\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown that commonly used kinase inhibitor selectivity definitions,
computed on a common log-affinity scale, split
three to one, into a distribution-based group whose members agree closely and a
ratio measure that agrees with none of them, and that the two measure
fundamentally different properties of binding profiles. This grouping is a
property of that common scale: under the association-constant form of the
original publication, the entropy does not cluster with the S-score and Gini.
We also showed that rank instability within and between families is
associated with binding profile shape, and that three
structurally distinct sources of instability can be identified and
characterized.
These findings motivate four properties that any well-formed selectivity measure
should satisfy: a reliability threshold
condition, bounded gap sensitivity, distributional consistency, and monotonicity
under weak off-target addition.

None of the four definitions assessed satisfies all four properties. The one
property all four violate is the reliability condition, a reporting convention
that any of them could adopt. The candidate we propose, a gated, smooth-hinge
effective-target number, satisfies all four: the reliability condition by a
reporting rule; distributional consistency because its activity boundary is
fixed at the assay floor (swept as a free parameter, that boundary moves the
ranking as much as the entropy's baseline does); and monotonicity under weak
off-target addition by its functional form, which we prove, and which the
entropy fails for a third of the Anastassiadis compounds. The candidate
recovers its full-panel ranking from roughly a third of a typical panel on
Klaeger and roughly a half on Metz, as the entropy does, and it is part of a
whole family of candidate definitions satisfying the four properties.
Because the candidate is a local modification of the entropy, the two rank the
compounds they both score almost identically (Spearman $+0.999$ on Klaeger), so
our candidate measure is neither unique nor an improvement on the entropy on
these data. The case for the properties therefore does not rest on these data:
they are requirements on every binding profile, and we propose them as checks
that any new selectivity measure should pass.

The main goal of this work is to identify criteria for judging 
a candidate that could be considered a principled, universally adopted 
choice of selectivity measure in the future. Such criteria must be justified 
(which we argue they are),
and jointly satisfiable (which the candidate demonstrates). 
Further properties may discriminate where these four do
not, and the candidate may separate from the entropy on panels whose active
fractions differ from the four studied here.
Establishing additional properties that uniquely characterize a selectivity
measure, as Shannon's axioms do for entropy, is a natural next step, for which
additional datasets may be required.

The immediate practical implication is that selectivity assessments should
report the sensitivity of rankings to parameter choice, and should explicitly
flag compounds with no binding above the activity threshold as unreliable.
The longer-term implication is that the field needs a principled basis for
choosing among selectivity definitions.
The need for a principled measure is not specific to selectivity. 
As future and ongoing work, we are developing a general
measurement-theoretic framework for defining and comparing the competing
measures of one concept, reporting where they agree and which pairs they
leave undecided, with kinase selectivity as one instance alongside cases
as varied as biological age and variant effect prediction.
