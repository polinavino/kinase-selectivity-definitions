\subsection{Existing Definitions Against the Properties}
\label{sec:desiderata}

Table~\ref{tab:desiderata} summarizes how the four definitions, three
comparators, and the candidate measure fare against the properties D1--D3
defined in Methods. None of them satisfies D2: moving the activity boundary
across $B$ reorders compounds under every measure in the table. D1 and D3
separate the measures. Simple constructions satisfy both: the active count, the
entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$, and the S-score with its threshold at the floor,
each reported only for compounds with an active target.

\textit{D1.} None of the four definitions has a reliability condition, so each
scores the 22 Klaeger compounds with no binding above the activity threshold.
Pooled across the four definitions' sweeps, their rank instability is
$\sigma = 77.9$ against $32.2$ for active compounds ($p < 0.001$, Mann--Whitney
$U$ test; Figure~\ref{fig:failure_modes}, left). Within a single definition the
contrast is significant only for the Gini ($65.0$ against $16.3$), through its
convention for undefined scores (Methods). It is small and not significant for
the entropy ($11.9$ against $12.6$, $p = 0.91$) and the ratio ($17.5$ against
$19.1$, $p = 0.51$), and reverses for the S-score ($19.0$ against $25.7$,
$p = 0.01$). The pooled contrast persists with the undefined entropy and Gini
configurations dropped ($66.2$ against $31.7$) and with only the S-score and
ratio pooled ($70.6$ against $37.7$, both $p < 0.001$). Computed only over each
compound's defined configurations, zero-active compounds are \emph{more}
stable than active ones within the entropy ($7.0$ against $13.5$) and the Gini
($6.7$ against $13.6$, both $p = 0.002$). The case for D1 is therefore not that
these scores are unstable within a definition, but that they rest on
sub-threshold values alone, which each definition treats by its own
convention. Any of the four definitions could satisfy D1 by withholding or
flagging such scores, as Bosc et al.\ did for the S-score.

\textit{D2.} Swept over $B = [x_0, \tau^*]$, the S-score's threshold reverses
the order of $8.4$--$13.0\%$ of pairs of scored compounds on Davis, Klaeger and
Anastassiadis, and $21.8\%$ on Metz. The entropy's and Gini's baselines reverse
$7.9$--$12.4\%$, and $22.5$--$22.8\%$ on Metz. Each comparator fixes a
parameter of one of these sweeps and so shares its sensitivity: the S-score with
$\tau = x_0$ and the active count reverse the same pairs as the S-score, and the
entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ the same pairs as the entropy. The candidate's
gate, swept with $T$ held at $\tau^* - x_0$, reverses $9.2$--$14.2\%$, and
$23.6\%$ on Metz. On Klaeger its per-compound instability is similar to that of the
entropy's baseline (mean rank $\sigma = 7.4$ and $7.5$;
Figure~\ref{fig:failure_modes}, right). For the ratio, the parameter is the
level $b$ in $R_b(k) = x_{(1)} - \max(x_{(k+1)}, b)$, which replaces any
off-target affinity below $b$, measured or not, by $b$. Moving $b$ across $B$
reverses $2.1$--$13.7\%$ of pairs on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, depending on
$k$, $0$--$2.6\%$ on Davis and $0.04$--$1.03\%$ on Metz (Supporting
Information, Table~S4). It exceeds $\varepsilon$ for every $k$ on Klaeger and
Anastassiadis. None of the measures in Table~\ref{tab:desiderata} therefore
satisfies D2, and the activity boundary must be fixed in the analysis protocol,
with its effect reported. The published entropy has no such boundary parameter
and is not assessed against D2. With its gate fixed,
the candidate's remaining parameter, its emphasis baseline, reverses at most
$0.72\%$ of pairs.

\textit{D3.} The S-score violates D3 for any threshold above the floor. Adding
a kinase bound between the floor and the threshold increases $N$ but not the
number of hits, so the hit fraction falls and the compound looks more
selective. With the threshold at the floor, every addition that D3 allows is a
hit. A compound with $C$ hits among $N$ kinases then moves from $-C/N$ to
$-(C+1)/(N+1)$, which is not higher, so the S-score with $\tau = x_0$ satisfies
D3. The active count ranks every panel exactly as the S-score at $\tau^*$ does
but does not divide by $N$, so it also satisfies D3.
D3 therefore concerns how scores change when a panel is extended, not how
compounds are ranked on a given panel. This matters whenever panels differ in
size or composition, as between Davis and Klaeger. The Gini violates D3 by
construction: it is computed over the full panel, so adding a weakly bound
kinase can raise its inequality. The ratio satisfies D3, because a sub-threshold kinase cannot displace the
primary target and cannot lower the reference off-target's affinity. The
entropy satisfies D3 at $\beta = \tau^*$, where no addition below $\tau^*$
carries weight, and for $\beta < \tau^*$ only for additions at or below $\beta$
(``Why the Smooth Hinge Matters'', below).

\subsection{The Candidate Measure}
\label{sec:candidate}

The candidate measure (Methods) is correctly oriented: its score falls with
the number of active kinases on every dataset (Spearman $-0.78$ to $-0.97$).
The non-binder gate is what makes this so. Without it, the non-binding kinases
at the floor dominate the profile and the score inverts, rising with the number
of active kinases ($r = +0.91$ on Klaeger).

Because the candidate changes only the entropy's gate and hinge, the two rank the
compounds they both score almost identically (Spearman $0.994$--$0.998$ on all
four datasets). The candidate satisfies D1 by its reliability gate and D3 by
its functional form (Methods, Proposition), while still weighting binding
between the floor and the activity threshold. It also ranks compounds almost
identically to the S-score with $\tau = x_0$ (Spearman $0.998$--$0.9997$), which
satisfies both properties too. What the candidate adds is a weight on each
target that increases continuously with affinity above the floor, which
separates compounds that a count of detected targets ties. Like every other measure, it
fails D2 when its gate is moved (above). The activity threshold $\tau^*$, which
sets its D1 gate and $T$, is also fixed. Moving it from $5.5$ to $7$ ($40\%$ to
$70\%$ on Anastassiadis) changes which compounds are scored but barely changes
how the candidate ranks those scored at both values (Spearman $\geq 0.999$ on
every dataset). D3 also holds for every scored compound in all four datasets when one
kinase is added at $\tau^*$, the strongest binding that D3 allows (Supporting
Information Table~S4).

Variants of the candidate behave in much the same way. Sweeping the emphasis
baseline reverses at most $\varepsilon$ of pairs for R\'enyi orders $0.5$ and
$1$ on Davis and Klaeger, and for smoothing widths from $0.75$ to $2$ times
$\tau^* - x_0$ on Klaeger. Order $2$ stays within $\varepsilon$ on Davis and
slightly exceeds it on Klaeger ($1.04\%$). D3 is proved only for the Shannon order,
$\alpha = 1$. These variants are reported in Supporting Information
(Section~S5).

\subsection{Recovery from Small Panels}
\label{sec:recovery}

Karaman et al.\cite{Karaman2008} scored each compound's S-score on random
subpanels and found that it varies more as panels shrink. Scoring every
compound on every subpanel, as they did, mixes the ranking with each
definition's convention for compounds that have no active target on the
subpanel (Supporting Information, Section~S1). Under D1, such a compound
receives no score. We therefore compared all measures on the same random
subpanels, from 10 targets up to the full panel, with the D1 gate applied to
all of them (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}). Coverage, the share of compounds
scored on the full panel that are still scored on the subpanel, is then the
same for every measure. At 50 targets it is $88\%$ on Davis, $71\%$ on Klaeger,
$77\%$ on Anastassiadis and $99\%$ on Metz. We write $p^*$ for the smallest
panel size tested at which the mean Spearman correlation with the full-panel
ranking, over compounds scored on both, exceeds $0.90$, a bar borrowed from the
``excellent'' range of intraclass-correlation guidelines.\cite{Koo2016}

How quickly a measure recovers depends on how much its ranking rests on the few
strongest targets (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}, top). On the full panel, the
S-score with $\tau = x_0$, the entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the
candidate all rank compounds by the share of targets with detected binding
(Spearman $\geq 0.98$ with the S-score at $\tau = x_0$ on every dataset). They
reach $p^*$ at 20 targets on Davis, 50 on Klaeger and 30 on Anastassiadis. The
S-score at $\tau^*$ and the active count need 30, 80 and 50, and the entropy
with $\beta = \tau^*$ 40, 100 and 65. The measures dominated by the strongest
targets recover slowest. The published entropy needs 300 of 363 targets on
Davis, 200 of 217 on Klaeger and 150 of 172 on Metz, and the ratio needs the
full panel on every dataset except Klaeger (200). The published entropy gives
binding below the activity threshold a small positive weight, so including weak
binding does not by itself make a measure recover quickly. On Metz, where
untested targets are set to the floor (Methods), the order of the first two
groups reverses (65 against 50 targets). The candidate recovers rankings no
better than the S-score with $\tau = x_0$.

The active count and the S-score at $\tau^*$ give identical recovery on every
dataset, although only the count satisfies D3. Recoverability is limited mainly
by coverage. Keeping $90\%$ of clearly separated pairs takes 150 targets on
Davis, 200 on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and 40 on Metz for every measure except
the ratio and the published entropy, which need as many or more
(Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}, middle). On Davis, Anastassiadis and Metz,
compounds whose score changes most when a single target is left out tend to
have larger rank errors at 50 targets (Spearman $r = 0.21$--$0.73$, for the
entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the candidate). On Klaeger this
association is weak ($r \leq 0.14$).

\subsection{Why the Smooth Hinge Matters}
\label{sec:hinge}

\textit{The entropy can violate D3.} Its hard cutoff gives a kinase just above
the baseline $\beta$ a weight close to zero. A profile with many such
kinases therefore has high entropy, and adding one kinase that is bound more
strongly, but still below $\tau^*$, concentrates the distribution and raises the
selectivity score. For example, with $\beta = 5.0$ and $\tau^* = 6.0$, a profile
with one kinase at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 7.0$ and fifty at $5.1$ has an
entropy-based selectivity of $-4.89$ bits, and adding one kinase at
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 6.0$ raises it to $-4.83$.
On Davis, Klaeger, and Metz, adding one kinase at $\tau^*$ never raises the
entropy's score for a compound with an active kinase, at any baseline in their
sweeps. On Anastassiadis it does at baselines below the $20\%$ floor. With raw
percent inhibition as weights ($\beta = 0\%$), it raises the score for $33\%$ of
the 133 compounds with an active kinase, and for $16\%$ at $\beta = 5\%$, but
for none at $15\%$ or above. Adding real kinases rarely does this: expanding
random 50- or 100-kinase panels by 1 to 30 kinases that a compound binds below
threshold raises the entropy's score in at most $0.1\%$ of cases. With
$\beta = \tau^*$, the entropy cannot violate D3, because it gives no weight to
binding below $\tau^*$.

\textit{The candidate cannot violate D3.} By the proposition in Methods, no
sub-threshold addition can raise its score, and its condition on $T$ is sharp:
just below it, a profile with one strong target and enough kinases just above
the floor violates D3. Below the bound, constructed profiles of this kind
violate D3 (by up to $0.014$ bits on Anastassiadis with $T = 10$), although none
of the real compounds does.
