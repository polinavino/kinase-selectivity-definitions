\subsection{Existing Definitions Against the Properties}
\label{sec:desiderata}

Table~\ref{tab:desiderata} summarizes how the four definitions, the two
comparators, and the candidate measure fare against the properties D1--D3
defined in Methods. No measure satisfies D2: wherever a parameter places the
activity boundary, moving it across $B$ reorders compounds. D1 and D3 separate
the measures, and simple constructions such as the active count and the entropy
with $\beta = \tau^*$ satisfy both.

\textit{D1.} None of the four definitions has a reliability condition, so each
scores the 22 Klaeger compounds with no binding above the activity threshold.
Pooled across the four definitions' sweeps, their rank instability is
$\sigma = 78.0$ against $32.1$ for active compounds ($p < 0.001$, Mann--Whitney
$U$ test; Figure~\ref{fig:failure_modes}, left). Within a single definition the
contrast is significant only for the Gini ($65.0$ against $16.0$), through its
convention for undefined scores (Methods). It is small and not significant for
the entropy ($11.8$ against $12.5$, $p = 0.81$) and the ratio ($17.3$ against
$19.2$, $p = 0.50$), and reverses for the S-score ($18.8$ against $25.6$,
$p = 0.01$). The pooled contrast persists with the undefined entropy and Gini
configurations dropped ($66.3$ against $31.6$) and with only the S-score and
ratio pooled ($70.7$ against $37.6$, both $p < 0.001$). Computed only over each
compound's defined configurations, zero-active compounds are \emph{more}
stable than active ones within the entropy ($6.9$ against $13.2$) and the Gini
($6.7$ against $13.3$, both $p = 0.002$). The case for D1 is therefore not that
these scores are unstable within a definition, but that they rest on
sub-threshold values alone, which each definition treats by its own
convention. Any of the four definitions could satisfy D1 by withholding or
flagging such scores, and $R(k)$ would under Bosc et al.'s convention (Methods,
``Ratio'').

\textit{D2.} Swept over $B = [x_0, \tau^*]$, the S-score's threshold reverses
the order of $8.4$--$13.0\%$ of pairs of scored compounds on Davis, Klaeger and
Anastassiadis, and $21.8\%$ on Metz. The entropy's and Gini's baselines reverse
$7.9$--$12.5\%$, and $22.5$--$22.8\%$ on Metz. The active count ranks every
panel as the S-score does, and the entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ is the entropy
at one end of its sweep, so both have the same sensitivity. The candidate's gate
reverses $9.2$--$14.2\%$, and $23.6\%$ on Metz, with per-compound instability
similar to that of the entropy's baseline on Klaeger (mean rank $\sigma = 7.3$
and $7.5$; Figure~\ref{fig:failure_modes}, right). The ratio's floor fallback
reverses $2.1$--$13.5\%$ of pairs on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, depending on
$k$, and up to $2.6\%$ on Davis. All are well above $\varepsilon = 1\%$. No
measure examined therefore satisfies D2, and the activity boundary must be
fixed in the analysis protocol, with its effect reported. With its gate fixed,
the candidate's remaining parameter, its emphasis baseline, reverses at most
$0.72\%$ of pairs.

\textit{D3.} The S-score and Gini violate D3 by construction: both are computed
over the full panel of size $N$, so adding a kinase the compound binds only
weakly lowers the S-score's hit fraction and can raise the Gini's inequality,
inflating the apparent selectivity. The active count ranks every panel exactly
as the S-score at $\tau^*$ does but does not divide by $N$, so it satisfies D3.
D3 therefore concerns how scores change when a panel is extended, not how
compounds are ranked on a given panel. This matters whenever panels differ in
size or composition, as between Davis and Klaeger.
The ratio satisfies D3, because a sub-threshold kinase cannot displace the
primary target and cannot lower the reference off-target's affinity. The
entropy satisfies D3 at $\beta = \tau^*$, where no addition below $\tau^*$
carries weight, and for $\beta < \tau^*$ only for additions at or below $\beta$
(``Why the Smooth Hinge Matters'', below).

\subsection{The Candidate Measure}
\label{sec:candidate}

The candidate measure (Methods) is correctly oriented: its score falls with
the number of active kinases on every dataset (Spearman $-0.78$ to $-0.97$).
The non-binder gate is what makes this so. Without it, the non-binding kinases
at the floor dominate the profile and the score inverts, rising with the number
of active kinases ($r = +0.91$ on Klaeger).

Because the candidate is a local modification of the entropy, the two rank the
compounds they both score almost identically (Spearman $0.994$--$0.998$ on all
four datasets). The candidate satisfies D1 by its reliability gate and D3 by
its functional form (Methods, Proposition), while still weighting binding
between the floor and the activity threshold. Like every other measure, it
fails D2 when its gate is moved (above). The activity threshold $\tau^*$, which
sets its D1 gate and $T$, is also fixed. Moving it from $5.5$ to $7$ ($40\%$ to
$70\%$ on Anastassiadis) changes which compounds are scored but barely changes
how the candidate ranks those scored at both values (Spearman $\geq 0.999$ on
every dataset). D3 also holds on every compound in all four datasets when one
kinase is added at $\tau^*$, the strongest binding that D3 allows (Supporting
Information Table~S4).

The featured candidate is one member of a family of measures: the R\'enyi
orders $0.5$, $1$ and $2$ keep the emphasis baseline within $\varepsilon$ on
Davis and Klaeger, as do smoothing widths from $0.75$ to $2$ times
$\tau^* - x_0$ on Klaeger. D3 is proved only for the Shannon order,
$\alpha = 1$. These variants are reported in Supporting Information
(Section~S5).

\subsection{Recovery from Small Panels}
\label{sec:recovery}

The panel-size analysis above scores every compound on every subpanel. Under
D1, a compound with no active target on a subpanel receives no score. We
therefore compared the four definitions, the two comparators and the candidate
on the same random subpanels, from 10 targets up to the full panel, with the
D1 gate applied to all of them (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate}). Coverage, the
share of compounds scored on the full panel that are still scored on the
subpanel, is then the same for every measure. At 50 targets it is $88\%$ on
Davis, $71\%$ on Klaeger, $77\%$ on Anastassiadis and $99\%$ on Metz.

Among the compounds scored on both panels, measures that weight binding below
the activity threshold recover the full-panel ranking from fewer targets on
three of the four datasets. The entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the
candidate reach mean Spearman $0.90$ at 20 targets on Davis, 50 on Klaeger and
30 on Anastassiadis. The S-score and the active count need 30, 100 and 50, and
the entropy with $\beta = \tau^*$ needs 40, 100 and 65. On Metz the order
reverses (65 against 50 targets), where untested targets are set to the floor
(Methods). The candidate is the only measure in the faster group that
satisfies D3, but it recovers rankings no better than the entropy with
$\beta = x_0$.

The active count and the S-score give identical recovery on every dataset,
although only the count satisfies D3. Recoverability, which counts a pair only
if both compounds are scored and keep their order, is limited mainly by
coverage. Keeping $90\%$ of clearly separated pairs takes 150 targets on Davis,
200 on Klaeger and Anastassiadis, and 40 on Metz for every measure except the
ratio, which needs as many or more. On Davis, Anastassiadis and Metz, compounds
whose score changes most when a single target is left out recover least well
(Spearman $r = 0.21$--$0.73$ between that change and the rank error at 50
targets, for the entropy with $\beta = x_0$, the Gini and the candidate). On
Klaeger this association is weak ($r \leq 0.14$).

\subsection{Why the Smooth Hinge Matters}
\label{sec:hinge}

The candidate and the entropy with $\beta = x_0$ differ in principle on D3,
through the candidate's functional form.

\textit{The entropy can violate D3.} Its hard cutoff gives a kinase just above
the baseline $\beta$ an arbitrarily small weight. A profile with many such
kinases therefore has high entropy, and adding one kinase that is bound more
strongly, but still below $\tau^*$, concentrates the distribution and raises the
selectivity score. For example, with $\beta = 5.0$ and $\tau^* = 6.0$, a profile
with one kinase at $\mathrm{p}K_d = 7.0$ and fifty at $5.1$ has an
entropy-based selectivity of $-4.89$ bits, and adding one kinase at
$\mathrm{p}K_d = 6.0$ raises it to $-4.83$.
On Davis, Klaeger, and Metz, adding one kinase at $\tau^*$ never raises the
entropy's score for a compound with an active kinase, at any baseline in their
sweeps. On Anastassiadis it does at baselines below the $20\%$ floor. With raw
percent inhibition as weights ($\beta = 0\%$), it raises the score for $33\%$ of
the 133 compounds with an active kinase, and for $16\%$ at $\beta = 5\%$, but
for none at $15\%$ or above. Adding real kinases rarely does this: expanding
random 50- or 100-kinase panels by 1 to 30 kinases that a compound binds below
threshold raises the entropy's score in at most $0.1\%$ of cases. With
$\beta = \tau^*$, the entropy cannot violate D3, because it gives no weight to
binding below $\tau^*$.

\textit{The candidate cannot violate D3.} By the proposition in Methods, no
sub-threshold addition can raise its score, and its condition on $T$ is sharp:
just below it, a profile with one strong target and enough kinases just above
the floor violates D3. With $T = 1$ on Metz or $T = 10$ on Anastassiadis, both
below the bound, constructed profiles of this kind violate D3 (by up to $0.014$
bits on Anastassiadis), although none of the real compounds does. Setting
$T = \tau^* - x_0$ (Methods) instead changes the Metz and Anastassiadis rankings
negligibly (Spearman $0.999$ against $T = 1$ and $T = 10$, respectively).
